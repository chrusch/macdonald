George MacDonald and the Problem of Evil

George MacDonald never wrote a treatise on the problem of evil. His fullest answer to it is a conversation in a novel. In chapter XLII of The Marquis of Lossie (1877), “St Ronan’s Well,” Malcolm, the young fisherman who is secretly the rightful marquis and is serving as groom to his own sister Florimel, reads Scott aloud to her and her friend Lady Clementina. A question of conduct in the story leads to a question about God, and Clementina, generous, radical and unbelieving, puts the oldest objection there is: how can a good God permit so much suffering?

Malcolm’s answer comes in stages, interrupted and pressed by Clementina at each step. This article follows the argument in order, quoting the chapter and saying what each passage contributes. The links go to the paragraphs on the George MacDonald site.

Two things are worth keeping in mind. First, Malcolm speaks for MacDonald: his teacher Mr Graham, whom he quotes throughout, is the novel’s voice of wisdom, and the narrator closes the chapter by vouching for the matter of what Malcolm says. Second, the argument is addressed to a person, not to the reading public. Its aim is not to win a debate but to help someone who would “gladly believe” if she could.

The question

The conversation reaches God by way of justice. Malcolm has argued that a man may not do a fresh wrong even to spare someone he loves, and Clementina asks whether a thing might be wrong “in the abstract, without having reference to any person.” Malcolm answers that there is no such abstract wrong:

There is no wrong man can do but is a thwarting of the living Right. Surely you believe, my lady, that there is a living Power of right, whose justice is the soul of our justice, who will have right done, and causes even our own souls to take up arms against us when we do wrong.

— ¶33

This sets the terms of everything that follows. MacDonald does not start from a God of power whose goodness then has to be defended. He starts from “the living Right,” a God whose justice is the source of ours and who is wronged “to the very heart” by injustice (¶35). The problem of evil is therefore a problem for such a God, not a problem about whether he cares.

Clementina’s reply is the objection in its classic form:

I would gladly believe in such a being, if things were so that I could. As they are, I confess it seems to me the best thing to doubt it. I do doubt it very much. How can I help doubting it, when I see so much suffering, oppression, and cruelty in the world? If there were such a being as you say, would he permit the horrible things we hear of on every hand?

— ¶36

MacDonald gives the objection its full weight. Clementina is not cynical. Her doubt comes from compassion, and from the very sense of justice Malcolm has just appealed to. The question is whether a just God is compatible with what justice itself condemns.

Comfort is not the highest good

Malcolm begins by admitting the difficulty was once his own, and then turns the question back on the questioner’s assumptions:

I used to find that a difficulty. Indeed it troubled me sorely until I came to understand things better. I remember Mr Graham saying once something like this—I did not understand it for months after: ‘Every kind-hearted person who thinks a great deal of being comfortable, and takes prosperity to consist in being well-off, must be tempted to doubt the existence of a God.—And perhaps it is well they should be so tempted,’ he added.

— ¶37

This is the hinge of the whole argument. The objection from suffering depends on an unstated premise: that comfort, ease and being well-off are the highest good. If they are, a God who withholds them is not good. Mr Graham does not call the doubter wicked. He calls him “kind-hearted,” and says the doubt is the natural result of a mistaken idea of what prosperity is.

The surprising addition, that it may be well for such people to be tempted to doubt, Malcolm explains next:

I think because such are in danger of believing in an evil God. And if men believed in an evil God, and had not the courage to defy him, they must sink to the very depths of savagery.

— ¶39

MacDonald would rather a person doubted God altogether than believed in a God who is not good. Unbelief is a lesser danger than worshipping power without goodness. Malcolm adds that the conversation with Mr Graham had been about “the Prometheus of Æschylus—how he would not give in to Jupiter” (¶41). Defiance of a bad god is better than submission to one. Clementina is startled: “Religious people, she found, could think as boldly as she” (¶40).

Malcolm then states the point directly:

If ease and comfort, and the pleasures of animal and intellectual being, were the best things to be had, as they are the only things most people desire, then that maker who did not care that his creatures should possess or were deprived of such, could not be a good God. But if the need with the lack of such things should be the means, the only means, of their gaining something in its very nature so much better that——

— ¶43

He concedes the objection completely on its own premise. The whole question turns on whether there is something “so much better” than comfort, and whether suffering can be the means to it.

A good worth the means

Clementina interrupts with the obvious reply: what if people don’t care about anything better, and are content as they are? Malcolm answers:

Should he then who called them into existence be limited in his further intents for the perfecting of their creation, by their notions concerning themselves who cannot add to their life one cubit?—such notions being often consciously dishonest? If he knows them worthless without something that he can give, shall he withhold his hand because they do not care that he should stretch it forth? Should a child not be taught to ride because he is content to run on foot?

— ¶45

Two ideas here run through all of MacDonald. Creation is not finished: God has “further intents for the perfecting of their creation,” and the life we have is the beginning of what he means to make of us. And the creature’s contentment is not the measure of its good. A parent does not stop teaching because the child is happy to stay as he is. The phrase “often consciously dishonest” is sharp: the claim to be content without anything higher is frequently not believed even by those who make it.

Clementina presses: “But the means, according to your own theory, are so frightful!” (¶46). Malcolm’s reply is the boldest claim in the chapter:

But suppose he knows that the barest beginnings of the good he intends them would not merely reconcile them to those means, but cause them to choose his will at any expense of suffering! I tell you, Lady Clementina… if I had not the hope of one day being good like God himself, if I thought there was no escape out of the wrong and badness I feel within me and know I am not able to rid myself of without supreme help, not all the wealth and honours of the world could reconcile me to life.

— ¶47

The argument here is not that suffering is worth it by some outside calculation. It is that the sufferers themselves, once they had tasted the good, would choose the means. The final judge of whether the suffering was justified is the one who suffered it. And the good in question is not a reward added afterwards. It is “being good like God himself,” escape from “the wrong and badness I feel within me.” This is the same good MacDonald names in the Unspoken Sermons as the end of all God’s dealings: deliverance from sin, not from its consequences.

Malcolm then supports the claim from his own heart. Clementina doubts he knows what he is talking about, and he answers that this desire has been his since childhood:

Some people are content not to do mean actions: I want to become incapable of a mean thought or feeling; and so I shall be before all is done.

— ¶56

“And so I shall be before all is done” is MacDonald’s universal hope stated in the first person. The confidence is not in himself but in the God who intends that end.

Not an explanation, but fair play

Clementina, troubled and half resentful, asks Malcolm to explain “how the God in whom you profess to believe can make use of such cruelties. It seems to me more like the revelling of a demon” (¶63). Malcolm refuses the task:

My lady! I never pretended to explain. All I say is, that, if I had reason for hoping there was a God, and if I found, from my own experience and the testimony of others, that suffering led to valued good, I should think, hope, expect to find that he caused suffering for reasons of the highest, purest and kindest import, such as when understood must be absolutely satisfactory to the sufferers themselves. If a man cannot believe that, and if he thinks the pain the worst evil of all, then of course he cannot believe there is a good God. Still, even then, if he would lay claim to being a lover of truth, he ought to give the idea—the mere idea of God fair play, lest there should be a good God after all, and he all his life doing him the injustice of refusing him his trust and obedience.

— ¶64

This is perhaps the most important passage in the chapter, because it shows what kind of argument MacDonald thinks is possible. He offers no theodicy, no account of why this child or that massacre. His claim is conditional and modest: if there is reason to hope for God, and if experience shows suffering leading to good, then it is reasonable to expect reasons that the sufferers themselves will find “absolutely satisfactory.” The evidence he appeals to is experiential: “my own experience and the testimony of others.”

The passage also turns the moral tables. The skeptic usually presents himself as the defender of justice against an unjust God. Malcolm points out that if there is a good God, the one who refuses him trust is doing him an injustice. A lover of truth must reckon with that possibility too.

Asked how to give the idea of God fair play, Malcolm answers in two steps:

By looking to the heart of whatever claims to be a revelation of him.

— ¶66

I will correct myself, and say—whatever of the sort has best claims on your regard—whatever any person you look upon as good, believes and would have you believe—at the same time doing diligently what you know to be right; for, if there be a God, that must be his will, and, if there be not, it remains our duty.

— ¶68

This is MacDonald’s constant method in miniature: obedience first, understanding after. The way to test the idea of God is not to survey every religion but to take the best light within reach and act on it. The last clause is a kind of wager, but an honest one. Doing right costs nothing whether God exists or not, so the practical counsel holds either way.

The narrator comments that Clementina’s unbelief is beginning to yield, and adds a sentence that states MacDonald’s view of skepticism:

There is no strength in unbelief. Even the unbelief of what is false is no source of might. It is the truth shining from behind that gives the strength to disbelieve.

— ¶70

Clementina’s doubt has force only because of the justice and compassion in her, and those come from the very God she doubts. Her objection is itself a witness to the “living Right.”

God suffers with his creatures

After a pause, Malcolm asks Clementina to “make your heart solemn” for something that came to him while thinking over her hard words to him in the park. It is the centre of his answer:

Everybody knows what few think about, that once there lived a man who… affirmed that he knew the secret of life, and understood the heart and history of men—who wept over their sorrows, yet worshipped the God of the whole earth, saying that he had known him from eternal days. The same said that he came to do what the Father did, and that he did nothing but what he had learned of the Father. They killed him, you know, my lady, in a terrible way that one is afraid even to think of. But he insisted that he laid down his life; that he allowed them to take it. Now I ask whether that grandest thing, crowning his life, the yielding of it to the hand of violence, he had not learned also from his Father. Was his death the only thing he had not so learned? If I am right, and I do not say if in doubt, then the suffering of those three terrible hours was a type of the suffering of the Father himself in bringing sons and daughters through the cleansing and glorifying fires, without which the created cannot be made the very children of God, partakers of the divine nature and peace. Then from the lowest, weakest tone of suffering, up to the loftiest pitch, the divinest acme of pain, there is not one pang to which the sensorium of the universe does not respond; never an untuneful vibration of nerve or spirit but thrills beyond the brain or the heart of the sufferer to the brain, the heart of the universe; and God, in the simplest, most literal, fullest sense, and not by sympathy alone, suffers with his creatures.

— ¶78

The argument moves in three steps.

The Son does only what he learns from the Father. This is the principle MacDonald draws from John 5:19 everywhere in his sermons: Jesus shows us the Father, and there is nothing in Jesus that is not first in God. Malcolm applies it to the one place where it is hardest to apply, the cross. If Jesus learned everything from the Father, he learned the laying down of his life from the Father too.

So the cross reveals the Father’s own suffering. The three hours are “a type of the suffering of the Father himself” in the whole work of making children out of creatures. The “cleansing and glorifying fires” are the suffering Clementina objects to. Malcolm does not deny them. He says that God is in them.

God suffers literally, not only by sympathy. Every pang “thrills beyond… the heart of the sufferer to… the heart of the universe.” This is the decisive change to the problem. The objection pictures God as a spectator who permits pain from outside. Malcolm’s God is not outside it. Whatever the creature suffers, God suffers with it. Whatever else may be said, he cannot be accused of imposing on others what he will not bear himself.

Malcolm’s “I do not say if in doubt” is worth noticing. The conditional is the form of the reasoning, not a sign of hesitation. It is the same confidence that runs through MacDonald’s universalism.

The Father-king, and the endurance that sustains

Clementina answers with the strongest objection in the chapter:

Well, but he is able to bear it; they are not: I cannot bring myself to see the right of it.

— ¶79

That God suffers with us shows he is not callous. It does not yet show he is right to permit the suffering, since his strength is not ours. Malcolm’s first reply simply restates his earlier ground: “Nor will you, my lady, so long as you cannot bring yourself to see the good they get by it” (¶80). Then he reminds her of an episode earlier in the novel, when she had condemned him for his harsh handling of his wild mare Kelpie:

You denied me a heart because of what seemed in your eyes cruelty. I knew that I was saving her from death at the least, probably from a life of torture: God may be good, though to you his government may seem to deny it. There is but one way God cares to govern—the way of the Father-king—and that way is at hand.

— ¶82

The Kelpie episode is MacDonald’s parable for the whole problem. Clementina saw the outside of an act and judged it cruel. Malcolm knew its purpose, and it was mercy. The point is not that every appearance of cruelty hides mercy, which Malcolm cannot show. It is that judging by appearances is unreliable precisely where the one acting knows more than the one watching. Earlier in the chapter Malcolm had said, of a different question, “The look of the thing is nothing” (¶14). “The way of the Father-king” names the kind of government MacDonald believes in: rule that is fatherly in its aim and royal in its power, never power for its own sake.

The parable is closer than it first appears, because Kelpie is in fact educated. In their first quarrel over the mare, Malcolm had told Clementina:

Horse or man—they never show kindness till they have learned fear. Kelpie would have torn me to pieces before now if I had taken your way with her. But except I can do a great deal more with her yet she will be nothing better than a natural brute beast made to be taken and destroyed.

— Chapter XXIV, ¶17

The last words are from 2 Peter 2:12, and the fate they name is real. A horse no one can manage is sold on, abused, or shot. Gentleness alone would have abandoned Kelpie to it, and Malcolm’s strong hand is all that stands between her and that end. And it works. Later “her submission to her master had been decidedly growing” (Chapter LI, ¶1). In the last chapter she grows “so much more gentle that at length Malcolm consented that Clementina… should mount her,” and she “ever after seemed proud of having a mistress that could ride” (Chapter LXXII, ¶39). The narrator credits part of the change to her foal, but the arc is plain. The mare who could not be saved by gentleness ends by gladly carrying the woman who had called her master cruel.

Then Malcolm gives what he calls “the other half” of his theory, and it answers Clementina’s objection more directly:

If God feels pain, then he puts forth his will to bear and subject that pain; if the pain comes to him from his creature, living in him, will the endurance of God be confined to himself, and not, in its turn, pass beyond the bounds of his individuality, and react upon the sufferer to his sustaining? I do not mean that sustaining which a man feels from knowing his will one with God’s and God with him, but such sustaining as those his creatures also may have who do not or cannot know whence the sustaining comes. I believe that the endurance of God goes forth to uphold, that his patience is strength to his creatures, and that, while the whole creation may well groan, its suffering is more bearable therefore than it seems to the repugnance of our regard.

— ¶82

Clementina had said God can bear it and his creatures cannot. Malcolm’s answer is that God’s bearing does not stay in God. The creature lives in God, so its pain reaches him, and his endurance flows back to it. This sustaining is not only for believers who know God is with them. It reaches “those… who do not or cannot know whence the sustaining comes”: animals, children, people broken by pain. These are the very cases that most trouble any account of suffering as discipline, because there the pain seems to teach nothing. Malcolm’s claim is not that their suffering is explained, but that they are not left alone in it.

The claim cannot be checked from outside. It can be checked from inside, by anyone who has found that a suffering which looked unendurable was nonetheless endured, and did not destroy them. That is the kind of testimony Malcolm appealed to earlier: “my own experience and the testimony of others.”

The allusion to Romans 8:22 (“the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together”) places all this within Paul’s hope for the deliverance of the creation.

Comfort for the pitiful

Clementina calls this “a dangerous doctrine” (¶83). Her fear is that if God upholds the sufferer, cruelty will be excused and compassion relaxed. Malcolm answers:

Will it then make the cruel man more cruel to be told that God is caring for the tortured creature from the citadel of whose life he would force an answer to save his own from the sphinx that must at last devour him, let him answer ever so wisely? Or will it make the tender less pitiful to be consoled a little in the agony of beholding what they cannot alleviate? Many hearts are from sympathy as sorely in need of comfort as those with whom they suffer. And to such I have one word more—to your heart, my lady, if it will consent to be consoled: The animals, I believe, suffer less than we, because they scarcely think of the past, and not at all of the future. It is the same with children, Mr Graham says; they suffer less than grown people, and for the same reason. To get back something of this privilege of theirs, we have to be obedient and take no thought for the morrow.

— ¶84

The first sentence seems aimed at vivisection, though the novel does not name it, and MacDonald opposed it elsewhere. The vivisector tortures a creature to force from its life an answer to the riddle of his own mortality, a riddle that will devour him however wisely he answers. Malcolm’s point is that the doctrine gives cruelty no cover: it says God is on the side of the tortured creature, not the torturer.

The rest of the passage changes the audience. Malcolm sees that Clementina’s objection is itself a form of suffering, the suffering of the compassionate who watch pain they cannot relieve. He speaks to that directly, and the shift is characteristic of MacDonald. The problem of evil is not only an intellectual puzzle to be solved. It is a wound in the hearts of good people, and it calls for consolation as much as argument.

The consolation is sound as far as it goes. A being that lives in the present cannot suffer the dread of pain to come, or pain projected into a future it has no conception of, and much human suffering is exactly of that kind. (MacDonald allows animals and children some life in the past, so the privilege is not complete.) The last sentence turns the observation into a way of life for us: to “take no thought for the morrow” (Matthew 6:34) is to win back something of their freedom through trust.

But the consolation speaks to how much such creatures suffer, not to why. For the why, the chapter points back to Kelpie. Malcolm’s main justification is that suffering is the means to a good the sufferer would choose, and Kelpie shows that this is not limited to beings who can reason about it. An animal too can be brought, through hardship it does not understand, to something better than it was, and to a bond with its master it would not exchange. What the parable cannot cover is suffering that ends before any such good appears: the creature broken or killed rather than bettered, the child who dies young. For them, the purpose Malcolm names would have to be fulfilled beyond this life. MacDonald says as much elsewhere, in his hope for the animals in “The Hope of the Universe,” but this conversation does not go there.

What the argument achieves

The chapter ends without resolution. “Clementina took up her work. Malcolm walked away” (¶85), and he declines to go on reading. The narrator then vouches for the substance of what he has said:

It may seem incredible that one so young as Malcolm should have been able to talk thus, and indeed my report may have given words more formal and systematic than his really were. For the matter of them, it must be remembered that he was not young in the effort to do and understand; and that the advantage to such a pupil of such a teacher as Mr Graham is illimitable.

— ¶88

“Not young in the effort to do and understand” puts doing first. Malcolm’s understanding of suffering is presented as the fruit of obedience, not of speculation.

Taken together, the chapter makes five moves:

  1. The objection rests on a premise. Suffering disproves a good God only if comfort is the highest good. If there is a good “so much better” and suffering can serve it, the bare fact of suffering proves nothing.
  2. The sufferers would choose the means. The final judges of whether the suffering was justified are those who suffered, once they have tasted the good intended for them.
  3. No explanation is offered. Malcolm “never pretended to explain.” He asks only that the idea of God be given fair play, by following the best light one has and doing what one knows to be right.
  4. God suffers with his creatures. The cross shows the Father’s own suffering. God is not a spectator of pain but shares it, literally.
  5. God’s endurance sustains the sufferer. His bearing flows back as strength, even to those who cannot know its source.

The first and fourth are the strongest. The first exposes the assumption the objection depends on. The fourth answers the charge that God is cruel by putting him inside the suffering rather than above it. The third is the most honest: it refuses to justify particular horrors, and makes faith a matter of trust and obedience rather than proof.

What the chapter leaves open is suffering that ends before it has done its work. Kelpie shows that Malcolm’s main justification reaches further than it first seems: animals too can be bettered by hardship they do not understand. But where the creature is broken or dies rather than bettered, the chapter offers God’s suffering with it, his endurance upholding it, and the lessening of its pain, without showing the good its suffering serves. Clementina’s objection, “he is able to bear it; they are not,” is answered for the rest of us by the fifth claim, which can be tested from inside by anyone who has suffered and not been destroyed. For those who were destroyed, it remains a hope.

That is where MacDonald leaves it, and it is consistent with everything else he wrote. The problem of evil is not solved by argument. It is lived through, in trust that the God shown in Jesus is the God who governs the world, and that “the way of the Father-king” is the only way he governs.
