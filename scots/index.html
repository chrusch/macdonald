Scots Words

With thanks to David Jack. If you would like to read these novels with their Scots put into English, David Jack has translated many of them, and his editions are warmly recommended: see his George MacDonald books.

Eleven of MacDonald’s novels give their people Scots to speak: the north-east speech he grew up with, the Doric of Aberdeenshire and Banffshire, with the fisher speech of the Moray coast in Malcolm and The Marquis of Lossie. This is every Scots word in them, 2,494 in all, gathered from 4,984 spellings that occur 114,481 times.

In those novels each Scots word is underlined with a faint dotted line: tap or click it for its meaning, and follow Full entry for the rest. The 100 commonest words, ye, ken, dinna, maun and their like, are left unmarked, because they come on nearly every page; they are on one page to learn first. The underlines can be turned off with the Scots button at the top of the page.

    A key to the spelling

    Much of MacDonald’s Scots is English words in Scots sounds, spelt as said. Once these patterns are familiar, most of them read at sight.

    How to read an entry

    • The count is every occurrence of every spelling, across the eleven novels. A count marked * is for a word that is also an ordinary English word (burn for a stream, mind for remember): it is counted, and underlined, only in passages of Scots speech, so the figure can still include a few English uses. A handful of the commonest (no, or, nor, till, ill, some, than, clean, fit) are so often the English word even in Scots speech that they are given no count and never underlined.
    • MacDonald glosses it gives the meaning MacDonald himself puts in brackets in the text, where he does, as in “a bowat (lantern)”. Those glosses are his, and the best authority there is.
    • The meaning is in the sense the novels use; where a word has several, the common ones come first.
    • Left out: standard English words, including the archaic English of the narrators (betwixt, hearken); English words with only a dropped final g (thinkin’), though a Scots stem (gaein’, speirin’) is here; names; and the Latin, German and Gaelic. The Highland English of Duncan MacPhail and other Gaelic speakers is not Scots, and has its own page.

    The glossary was drawn up with the help of AI from the texts on this site, word by word, and checked against MacDonald’s own glosses; the counts and examples are taken afresh from the novels each time the site is built.
