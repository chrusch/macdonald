Second Series

The Hardness of the Way

Modern English paraphrase. MacDonald's text restated paragraph by paragraph in present-day English. Read the original.

The passage: “Children, how hard it is!” (St. Mark x. 24.)

I suspect nearly every thoughtful young rich man feels the Lord treated this young man harshly. He asks why the rich should find it hard to enter the kingdom of heaven and fare differently from everyone else in the world to come, taking a fact of nature for an arbitrary decree born of some prejudice in God or some objection to his creatures’ enjoyment. He fails to see that this is the law: they will fare like everyone else, when what they want is to fare as rich people. I can’t imagine heaven’s kingdom being easy for a rich man to enter: every earthly kingdom lets the rich in, and puts them first if they are rich enough. A rich man isn’t bound to belong to Satan’s kingdom, but he is especially welcome there, while heaven welcomes him just as it welcomes anyone.

I suspect too that many a rich man resents this story’s claim on everything he has, and that many people, rich or not, can’t believe the Lord really meant to take the poor fellow’s money. To a man born rich, wealth seems natural and essential to well-being; a man who made his own feels it is his through labour by day and worry by night. Each feels entitled to what he holds, so it seems hard that this right should collide with the necessity of entering heaven’s kingdom. Why can’t he have the best of both worlds? He would compromise if he could, serving Mammon a little and God a lot, and gaining something of heaven at the least cost to the world. But he doesn’t mean the best that comes from making the lower serve the higher, giving up this world’s treasure for heaven’s. What he wants of heaven isn’t its best, and what he won’t give up of the world is its worst.

I can easily picture an honest young man, brought up in Christian ways, reasoning like this. “Does this story apply to everyone? Must I give up everything if I become a Christian? That was easy then, and I’d have done it if the Saviour himself had asked. But wealth meant something different socially then; now I’d give up far more. Nor do I love money as he was in danger of doing; the Jews have always worshipped Mammon. I try to do good with mine. Besides, I’m a Christian already, so why ask the same of me as of a young Jew? If everyone with a conscience about money gave it all up, the irreligious would hold the power unopposed and the world would go to the devil. The Bible often mentions rich men but never tells another to part with everything. Ananias was struck dead for pretending to give all, not for keeping some, and Peter said the land was his own until sold, and the money his afterwards. How would the Lord have been buried without the rich Joseph? He did say, ‘If you want to be perfect, go and sell what you have,’ but I can’t be perfect; it’s hopeless, and he doesn’t expect it.” It would be more honest to say, “I don’t want to be perfect; I’m content to be saved.” Such people don’t care about being perfect as their Father in heaven is perfect, only about being what they call saved, and little think that perfection is salvation: the two are one. “And besides,” he concludes in triumph, “the text says, ‘How hard it is for those who trust in riches to enter the kingdom of God!’ I don’t trust in my riches. I know they can’t save me.”

Suppose I were speaking with such a young man. I would state a truth only as a siege to win his surrender to the law of liberty; if I couldn’t persuade him, I’d keep quiet. I wouldn’t labour to instruct the keenest mind; I’d rather learn. Only persuading the heart, will and actions deserves a man’s full energy, first for his own manhood, then his neighbour’s: the beam out of his own eye before the speck out of his brother’s, if that speck is more than the end of his own beam. Making someone happy as a lark might be doing him serious wrong; making him wake up, rise, look up and turn is worth the life and death of the Son of the Eternal.

So I say to the young man:

“Have you been keeping the commandments?”

“I wouldn’t dare say that,” I imagine him answering. “I should know better than that young man how much the commandments demand.”

“But does that answer mean,” I press, “that you think the Lord a hard master and so have tried less to please him? Or that you have strained toward his perfect standard and seen that the law can’t be fulfilled? Surely you’ve noticed that the shocking command to give up everything came to someone who had kept the commandments for years. Have you kept them as he did, so that it could come to you? Dissatisfied with your keeping and longing to be perfect, have you gone on your knees to the Master to learn more of the way to eternal life? Or are you so content with yourself that you’ve never hungered and thirsted for God’s righteousness, the perfection of your being? If so, take comfort: the Master doesn’t ask you to sell what you have and give to the poor. You follow him, you preach good news with him, when you don’t care about righteousness! You’re not the company he wants, and he won’t ask for your purse; it’s nothing to him whether you give or keep. Is he to be obliged for money to someone outside his kingdom, false and ignoble? Bring him a true heart and an obedient hand, which he gave his life’s blood for.”

“Please don’t be harsh. I admit I haven’t been what I should, but I want to repent and enter into life. Don’t take my reluctance to throw everything away, without knowing it’s required, as disregard for higher things.”

“Then once more: go and keep the commandments. It hasn’t come to your money yet; you aren’t yet a child in the kingdom, caring for your father’s arms and not just the shelter of his roof. Let the commandments tell you how to use your money; it is pitiable presumption to wonder whether you must sell it all. Wait until keeping them has shown you the great reward of loving what’s right, then that for all your effort you’re only an unprofitable servant; until you know that only those who need no law can keep it, and would rather cease to be than live the poor, selfish life that is all you can call your own; until you sense something beyond your mind’s thought but not your heart’s longing, something not yours, seemingly never to be yours, without which your life is worthless. When all this brings you to the Master crying, ‘What must I do to inherit eternal life?’, he may say, ‘Sell all you have, give to the poor, and come follow me.’ Obey then and you’ll be most honourable; refuse and you’ll be most pitiable. Until then you’d be no comfort to him and no pleasure to his friends. For the young man, selling all and following him would have meant accepting a patent of nobility from God; it isn’t offered to you. If one of the disobedient gave up every straw he owned in hope of that honour, he’d just be sent back to keep the commandments in the easier circumstances of poverty.

“Does that comfort you? Alas for you! Your relief is that the Lord has no need of you, doesn’t require your money, and doesn’t offer you himself instead! You haven’t sold him for thirty pieces of silver, but you’re glad not to buy him with all you have. How do you differ from the young man in the story? He was invited to do everything, to share the divine nature; you never had the chance to refuse, because you aren’t fit to be invited. People like you can never enter the kingdom. You wouldn’t know you were in heaven if you were there.”

“But I don’t trust in my riches. I trust in the merits of my Lord and Saviour, in his finished work and the sacrifice he offered.”

“Yes, yes! You’ll trust in anything but the Man himself, who tells you it’s hard to be saved. All the merits of God and his Christ can’t give you eternal life; only God and his Christ can, and they can’t, and wouldn’t if they could, unless you keep the commandments. Knowing the living God is eternal life; what you need is not his merits but him. As for trusting in riches, who with even half a conscience or half a head could imagine that anyone relying on them to get him in could enter? A Jew confident in his wealth might hope it signalled God’s favour that wouldn’t fail him, or be too self-satisfied to imagine being lost; but trusting in his riches? No. Saying he doesn’t trust in them to take him into life is the last refuge of the money-worshipper, the man who needs wealth for his peace. The man who thinks of nothing else does trust in it, in a fearful sense; yet hundreds who do will say, ‘I don’t trust in my riches; I trust in…’ some stock phrase.”

“You’re forgetting yourself. You’re criticizing the Lord’s own words: he said, ‘How hard it is for those who trust in riches to enter the kingdom of heaven!’”

“I’m not. I believe our Lord never said those words. The two oldest manuscripts, the Sinaitic and the Vatican, read, ‘Children, how hard it is to enter the kingdom of God!’ I’m glad Westcott and Tischendorf prefer that reading, though Tregelles and the Revisers don’t. I take it to be the Lord’s. Some copyist with a rich man’s mind, uneasy at the Lord’s view of money and eager like you to compromise, added a marginal note: it isn’t having riches but trusting in them that makes entering hard! Hard? For a man who trusts in riches it is eternally impossible; it is the man who merely has them for whom it is hard. Is the Lord supposed to teach that a man who trusts in riches can enter, that this is impossible for men but possible with God? He never said it. Mr. Facingbothways’s annotation crept into the text and stands in the English version. Our Lord didn’t explain away his hard words; he let them stand as the burning fire meant to purge us. Where simplicity meets simplicity they are understood; the double heart must mistake. It is hard for a rich man, simply because he is rich, to enter the kingdom of heaven.”

Some comfort themselves that God will take the difficulty into account, another form of the notion that a rich man, having had his good things here, must have them there too. They’ll certainly get fairness, but what good is that if they don’t enter the kingdom? It is life they need; existence without it can’t be endured, yet they think they can do without eternal life if only they live for ever! Those who know what eternal life is count it the one terror to live on without it.

Take the Lord’s words, then, as he spoke them: “Children, how hard it is to enter the kingdom of God!” He first has them reflect on how hard it is for anyone to enter, then restates the rich man’s difficulty more strongly: “It is easier for a camel to go through a needle’s eye than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God.” It always has been and will be hard to enter; it is hard even to believe one must be born from above, passing into a new, unknown consciousness. The law-abiding Jew and the ceremonial Christian shrink from self-annihilation, from the life of grace and truth, from the high clear air of heavenly delight, and from the all-embracing love that fulfils the law and sets it aside; they can’t accept a state of being as itself eternal life. What is hard to believe in is hard to enter. How hard? As hard as the Master could put it: “If any man comes to me and does not hate … his own life also, he cannot be my disciple.” A rich man finds this harder still, since so much is tied to his life to swell his sense of self that casting it off as the ugly shadow of the self God made is far harder.

Nobody knows how hard it is to enter the kingdom of heaven except those who have tried hard and not stopped trying. Don’t tell me one can pass at once into full assurance; I want not the certainty of eternal life but eternal life. Whatever other preachers say, in St Paul the spirit and flesh often fought. Only those who want life simply as eternal, and struggle every day and soon every hour, know how hard entering is; they see that no moment is life without the presence that strengthens. I don’t deny anyone’s testimony of peace and unspeakable joy as the instant result of the new birth; I say only that if by salvation they mean less than absolute oneness with God, it’s no salvation to me, and I wouldn’t be content with it with every joy of heaven thrown in. If they aren’t righteous as he is righteous, they aren’t saved, however glad; they are only on the way. If they don’t love their neighbour, not “as themselves” (a phrase hard to understand and not Christ’s) but as Christ loves him, I can’t count them entered into life, though life may have begun to enter them. The disciples little knew what the Lord’s “how hard it is to enter the kingdom” would ask of them.

New demands keep coming to the Christian; the Spirit’s ever-fresh calling is what works in the children of obedience. When he thinks he has arrived, he is in danger. When the mountain he has long climbed suddenly shows a distant peak, radiant white and nearly lost in heaven, that no human foot seems able to reach, then there is hope: it proves he has been climbing, since he sees what he couldn’t before. He may know little of what he is, but he learns again and again that he isn’t in the world as Jesus was; and the very wind that gives him courage as he climbs is the hope that one day he will be like him, seeing him as he is.

Possessions are things, and things, except as something to conquer and a means of spiritual gain, readily turn against the better life. Whoever depends on anything but life itself for his sense of well-being is a slave, hanging on something less than himself. A person isn’t perfect unless he could sit calmly content with every thing taken from him, aware of a well-being untouched, still possessing all things as a child of the Eternal. Things, our bodies first, are given to train us in independence of them and true possession of them: we must own them, not be owned. They are meant to mediate, as lower shapes of unseen things that belong to the unshakeable world of silence and being, not speech and showing. Those unseen things take form in time and space not to exist, since they exist in eternal Godhead, but to be known by those in training for the eternal, and God’s sons and daughters must possess them. Instead people grasp at the forms, treating the visible as what is to be owned and loving bodies rather than souls. Some good people can hardly believe that if the young man had given up his wealth, the Lord wouldn’t have told him to keep it; they think heaven’s treasure a poor substitute. “Isn’t wealth power?” they ask. It is, and so is a wolf hiding in a robe: the power of a brute machine whose levers and valves its owner hardly understands. Most readers of the story agree with the young man at his worst moment, as he turned away, except that they aren’t sorrowful.

Nobody can really possess things who can’t do without them, who wouldn’t be perfectly content in the knowledge that the cause of his being is present in his consciousness and with him. To be clear: anyone with God’s presence is content. What I mean is that only someone who has the Father, and is eternally content in him alone, can possess a sunset, a field of grass, a gold mine or a person’s love as God means him to. Whoever has God has all things, as their maker has them. To every man, woman and child I say: if you aren’t content, it’s because God isn’t with you as you need him, more than your body ever needed food or air, more than your soul ever hungered for joy or peace.

We must get free of the tyranny of things. See how urgent it is. However tightly the young man clings to his wealth, God will do what he can: his child won’t be left in the hell of possession. When the angel of death comes, where are the things that haunted the poor soul? The world and all in it slip from his hands and feet, taking his body, eyes and ears, and every purse and coffer that deluded him into thinking he owned anything.

“If death frees a man from the rule of things, why hurry it? Shouldn’t the young man wait for the Inevitable to free him?”

Not so! I presume that only then does the man of things see their tyranny. Abstaining first shows a man his craving’s strength; having nothing left may first show him how much he made a necessity of things. If he then fights them, casting out of his soul what death tore from his hands, he will first know the full passion of possession, the slavery of prizing the worthless part of the precious.

“Then what good is Death? He removes the sting but leaves the poison!”

This: it’s not the fetters that chafe but the fetters that soothe that eat into the soul. When the golden fetters he loved to look at are gone, though they held him fast to his dungeon wall, he will first feel the soreness of their loss, in a weary indifference to earth and sea, space and stars. When it dawns on him that they were a horror and a disgrace, the good of saving death will appear, and he will begin to understand that having never was well-being: we don’t live by possessing but possess by living. So losing what he thought he had moves him, hardly toward deliverance but in its favour: it may feel like the start of slavery when it is the start of freedom. No soul was ever freed without feeling its slavery; nothing but itself can enslave a soul, nothing outside itself free it.

Take the drunkard, freed from his body but keeping his craving, unable to indulge it. With no means left to drown thought, he finally has time to think; surely a fearful hope dawns on him then! But he isn’t saved until, by God’s power and his own obedient effort, he would refuse the temptation even for an eternity of drunkenness with all its delights and none of its penalties.

So death may give a new opportunity, with some hope for the multitude who count themselves Christians yet are possessed by things as by a legion of devils: respected in their church, thought blameless, kind and generous, believers in Jesus’s redemption, yet whose care is always to heap up more, adding house to house and field to field, burying themselves deeper in the ash-heap of Things.

But things rule not only the rich man; people with no money are slaves too, if lack of it makes them unhappy. The man forever digging his grave is little better than the one already rotting in it, and the money one has and the other wants causes the same enduring stupidity in each. To both comes the word: “How is it that you do not understand?”
