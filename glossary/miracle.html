A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

miracle

miracles · wonder-working

Frequency in the non-fiction: miracle and miracles 741, nearly all in The Miracles of Our Lord; miraculous 6. Elsewhere chiefly in “The Temptation in the Wilderness”.

In brief. A miracle, for MacDonald, is not a breach of nature but a work of the Father done by the Son quickly and in plain view, an epitome of God’s processes in nature seen in immediate connection with their source. The miracles show the will of God at work behind the long gradations of law, hasten what God ordinarily does over years, restore the original order of humanity, and fulfil nature’s deepest laws. Jesus never worked one for himself. Their value lay in revealing the Father’s care to those who could not otherwise see it, and above all in their effect on the people healed.

Shades of meaning

  1. An epitome of nature. “This, I think, is the true nature of the miracles, an epitome of God’s processes in nature beheld in immediate connection with their source—a source as yet lost to the eyes and too often to the hearts of men in the far-receding gradations of continuous law. That men might see the will of God at work, Jesus did the works of his Father thus” (Miracles, “Introduction”). “What in the hands of the Father are the mighty motions and progresses and conquests of life, in the hands of the Son are miracles” (same). See nature and Father.

  2. Less than nature beheld at its heart. “The miracles are mightier far than any goings on of nature as beheld by common eyes, dissociating them from a living Will; but the miracles are surely less than those mighty goings on of nature with God beheld at their heart” (same). The miracles in bread and wine “were far less grand and less beautiful than the works of the Father they represented, in making the corn to grow in the valleys” (same). See growth, sense 8.

  3. A hastening. “There was in these miracles, and I think in all, only a hastening of appearances; the doing of that in a day, which may ordinarily take a thousand years, for with God time is not what it is with us” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). The bread and fish of the feeding were not changed from what God made them, as the stone would have been.

  4. Higher law, not broken law. God “might stop the working of one law by the intervention of another. Such intervention, if not understood by us, would be what we call a miracle” (Miracles, “The Government of Nature”). Seen at their heart, the miracles show “a possible fulfilment of her deepest laws” (Miracles, “Introduction”). The cures are “for the setting right of what has gone wrong, the restoration of the order of things,—namely, of the original condition of humanity” (Miracles, “The Government of Nature”). See law, sense 7.

  5. Never for himself. “As he refused to make stones bread, so throughout that life he never wrought a miracle to help himself” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). “No miracle was needful for himself: he saw the root of the matter—the care of God. But he revealed this root in a few rare and hastened flowers to the eyes that could not see to the root” (Miracles, “The Government of Nature”). See temptation.

  6. The love in them. The lesson of the loaves was that God cared for his children: “No doubt the power of the miracle was some proof of his mission, but the love of it proved it better, for it made it worth proving: it was a throb of the Father’s heart” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Cause of Spiritual Stupidity”). See stupidity and care · anxiety.

  7. Not compelling faith. “Wonderful works can only nourish a faith already existent” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Jesus and his Fellow Townsmen”). “It is possible to see a miracle, and not believe in it; while many of those who saw a miracle of our Lord believed in the miracle, and yet did not believe in him” (Miracles, “The Beginning of Miracles”). “The miracles were for the persons on whom they passed … Their tongues could but witness to a fact; their lives could witness to a truth” (Miracles, “Miracles of Healing Unsolicited”). See faith and truth.

  8. A symbol of glorifying all things. The first miracle, water into wine, “is a true symbol of what he has done for the world in glorifying all things. With his divine alchemy he turns not only water into wine, but common things into radiant mysteries, yea, every meal into a eucharist, and the jaws of the sepulchre into an outgoing gate” (Miracles, “The Beginning of Miracles”). Destruction also has its miracle, the fig tree being “but the acted parable” (Miracles, “Miracles of Destruction”). See symbol.

Neighbouring words

  • works of the Father (what they are). Sense 1.
  • nature, law (their setting). Senses 2 and 4; see nature and law.
  • sign, wonder (their outward aspect). Sense 7.
  • symbol, parable (their meaning). Sense 8.
  • care of God (their root). Senses 5 and 6.
  • resurrection (the chief of them). The Resurrection “miracle it was, as certainly as any of our Lord’s, whatever interpretation be put upon the word” (Miracles, “The Raising of the Dead”).
  • fact (their status). “Either the miracles are fact, or I lose—not my faith in this man—but certain outward signs of truths which these very signs have aided me to discover” (Miracles, “The Government of Nature”).

Scripture. John 2:11, This beginning of miracles did Jesus in Cana of Galilee (sense 8); John 5:17, 19, My Father worketh hitherto, and I work … the Son can do nothing of himself, but what he seeth the Father do (sense 1); Matthew 4:3–4, command that these stones be made bread (senses 3 and 5); Mark 8:17–21, the leaven and the loaves, the text of “The Cause of Spiritual Stupidity” (sense 6).
