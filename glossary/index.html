A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

The words that carry the weight of MacDonald’s thought, and what he means by them. Many are ordinary words, obedience, Father, imagination, justice, that he uses in a sense of his own.

Each entry gives the meaning in brief, then its shades of meaning, each shown in MacDonald’s own words, with every quotation linked to the paragraph it comes from in these editions. Most come from Unspoken Sermons, The Hope of the Gospel, The Miracles of Our Lord and A Dish of Orts; where the novels and poems use a word in a telling way, the entry says so. It ends with the words he defines it with or against, and the passages of Scripture behind it. The counts at the head of an entry are from those four books.

The entries, in brief

artwork of art · artist · fairytale
Art is human making in forms that God has already made. The artist invents no material of his own, so his work always means more than he intended, while God’s work means exactly what he meant. A true work of art does not carry one meaning to be decoded. It wakes meanings in whoever meets it. A fairytale is the freest kind of art: a little world with its own laws, which in physical things may be invented but in moral things must obey. Art is one of God’s good gifts, and nothing a man must forsake to follow Christ, though it may be made an idol for the praise of men.
atonementat-one-ment · sacrifice · vicarious sacrifice
MacDonald refuses the atonement as his questioner means it, a satisfaction paid to God’s justice by a substitute, and calls that doctrine a lie which keeps men from the gospel. He accepts the word in its older English sense of at-one-ment: Jesus is our atonement because through him we are reconciled to God, not God to us. Atonement is a making-up and a bringing together, which only the sinner himself can make, and Christ is its prince, who makes us make it. Sacrifice likewise is turned round. God is not the receiver of sacrifice but its giver: he has always been sacrificing himself for his creatures, and the whole life of Jesus was the offering of himself to the Father. Men’s sacrifices were their own invention, borne with until they could learn better. The true sacrifice asked of us is the self and the will, given back to God in freedom. Vicarious sacrifice and imputed righteousness he calls legal fiction.
beautybeautiful · loveliness
Beauty is for MacDonald the form truth takes when it is seen. God is the first of artists, who has put beauty into nature so that it may affect us; the beauty of a thing is the final cause of its existence; and God himself is beautiful with a beauty that creates beauty. Beauty is the garment of truth and cannot exist without it, and so mere prettiness, ornament or effeminate sweetness is not beauty at all. The highest poetic feeling springs less from perfected beauty than from creation’s longing for what is not yet.
beingexistence · self-existent
What a creature or God fundamentally is, as distinct from what he does, feels or knows. MacDonald uses being in three linked ways. God is being itself, the only self-existent. A man’s being is the deepest layer of himself, below consciousness and below his acts, where only God can work. That being is meant to come into its truth, and the man must will it. Existence is often the lower word: bare continuance, which without life or right is not worth having.
carecares · anxiety · thought for the morrow
Care, in MacDonald, is anxious fretting over provision: thought for the morrow, or for the next minute, which is equally beyond our reach and equally in God’s hands. It is distrust in its daily form, and distrust is atheism. Care fills the mind so that it cannot understand the Lord, as the disciples could not, worrying about one loaf after two miracles of bread. Only forethought that turns into present duty is right. Care is not a better spur to work than the call of God, and every care should be taken straight to him.
childson · sonship · childlike
Every human being is God’s child by being made. To become a son or daughter is something more: to grow up into the Father’s likeness by willing it, as Jesus did. Sonship is therefore both the end of creation and the content of salvation. Childlike names something different again, not immaturity but a quality of the divine nature itself, which the child shows and the grown man must recover.
churchchurches · church-going · the true church
MacDonald’s church is first of all the people the Lord heals and claims: its foundation was laid in his miracles, it is the purer family within the larger, and it grows like leaven. The church as an institution gets hard words. It is not the place of divine service, which is done in the world, in serving the neighbour. Its great heresy is to put something other than obedience in the place of faith, and to disbelieve in the Spirit’s teaching of each man. Its main fault is separation: those who tear the body of Christ apart for the sake of uniformity, and those who care more for the church than for Christ.
conscience
The inward witness to right and wrong, which MacDonald treats as the voice of God in a man and as his final court of appeal. It is the faculty Christ’s teaching is addressed to, more than the intellect. No authority, however good, may require a man to believe of God what his conscience calls evil. Conscience is also the point where obedience starts: it always tells a man what to do next.
consciousnessconscious · self-consciousness
Awareness, and especially awareness of being. MacDonald treats consciousness as a growing thing that God means to enlarge without limit, and as the place where God’s presence is felt. It is not life itself, only its outcome; and the consciousness of self is far less than the self, which God knows better. The passage from consciousness to self-consciousness is a second birth, but self-consciousness can also be diseased, a turning inward that needs to give way to the consciousness of what God thinks of us.
consuming firefire of God
MacDonald’s name for the love of God as it meets what is impure, taken from Hebrews 12:29. The fire is not a second thing in God beside his love; it is the love itself, which destroys sin and spares the sinner. It is felt as torment by those who hold on to their sin and as light or comfort by those who come near.
deathdie · dead
A negation, not a power. MacDonald’s death has two sides. Bodily death is a passing, a release and a renewal of ties, which the God of the living cannot let be final. The real death is the self-centred life cut off from God, and the way out of it is another dying, the death to self that Christ died. The opposite of death is not survival but life, and both words are best read with life.
devilSatan · the evil one · devils · possession
MacDonald speaks of Satan as a real adversary of truth, humanity and God, the lord of misrule, but he gives him little room. He reads the temptation in the wilderness as a parable told by Jesus, not as the visit of a visible demon who quoted Scripture, and he finds the substance of serving Satan in very human things: knowing better and doing worse, leading men toward an end that is not the best, flattering a nation’s pride. He keeps apart the question of possession from the question whether a personal devil exists, and he thinks the old account of possessing spirits fits the Gospel records better than disease alone, though he will not be dogmatic. Men make themselves the devil’s children by dividing and separating; whoever destroys the devil’s power is on Christ’s side. Torture is what one expects of Satan, healing what one expects of God. Satan is foiled when a man joins God.
doubtdoubts · doubting · honest doubt
MacDonald does not treat doubt as the enemy of faith. Doubts are the messengers of the Living One, sent to rouse the honest; they are the first knock of truths not yet understood, and every deeper assurance must be preceded by them. A man may be haunted by doubts and grow by them in faith. What matters is the will behind the doubt. Childlike doubt, ready to believe if it can, is dear to God and will feed the faith at its heart; the doubt of a man who would only use certainty to go on disobeying receives no answer. The worst doubt is not of God’s existence but of his goodness, and doubt is answered not by argument but by obedience and the sight of Jesus.
dutyduties
What is owed, and the first thing to be done. MacDonald gives duty two values. It is imperative and the only road forward: the duty of the moment is the door to light, and the man who has begun to recognize it is on the path of life. But duty done only as duty is a slave’s righteousness, which must give way to love. Duty is the way to freedom, and freedom is what lies beyond duty.
essentialessence · essential life
Essential in MacDonald most often means what a thing truly is, as against its accidents, and above all what God is. God’s essential being is love, his fire, his life; Christ is the essential Life and men are made to share in essential being. So essential attaches to life, joy, love, humanity, childhood and bliss to mark the real thing, the thing God means, beneath the blurred form we see. It also keeps its common sense of what cannot be done without.
eternaleternal life · eternity
For MacDonald eternal names a kind of life, not a length of time. Eternal life is knowing God, oneness with him, life “come awake”; it is God’s own life shared by children who do his will. Mere endless existence without it would be “worthless as hell”. He therefore cares little for the certainty of eternal life and much for the thing itself, and he speaks of eternal facts, eternal hunger and eternal death as qualities of being. God himself is the Eternal, and Jesus came to give men God, who is eternal life.
faithtrust · believe
Trust in God himself, shown in doing what he says. For MacDonald faith is personal before it is propositional: it is in a person, not in statements, even true statements, about him. It is the same thing as obedience seen from the inside, and it grows into knowledge. Belief in the sense of holding an opinion is a lesser thing and often its counterfeit.
Fatherfatherhood
God’s own name, and for MacDonald the truest one: God is the Father, first of the eternal Son and then of every man. Fatherhood is not one attribute among others, or an office God holds beside that of judge; it is what he is, and every other word about God must be read through it. The human father is its copy, not its model.
fearafraid · terror · dread
The dread a creature feels before a power it does not know, or knows only as a demand it cannot meet. MacDonald does not despise this fear. It is natural and, for the man far from God, reasonable. But it belongs to imperfect love and distance, and only love can cast it out. Its cure is not less of God but more: the nearer presence of the very Power that is feared.
flowerblossom · primrose · rose · daisy · gowan · snowdrop
A flower is God’s thought, sent as a gift of himself to the eyes and hearts of his children. Its truth is not the facts of its botany but the flower itself and what it cannot help saying. Blossom is MacDonald’s favourite figure for the completion of a thing, the end it was made for. So the truth of a thing is its blossom, Christ is the blossom of humanity, and each soul is a distinct flower in God’s garden, named by what it will become. The named flowers each carry their own meaning. The primrose stands for shy, careful trust. The rose is the full summer of Paradise. The daisy, the Scots gowan, is the common flower of childhood and the meek. The snowdrop is the striving, hoping Earth, waiting under the snow for the resurrection.
forgivenessforgive · unforgiving · remission
Forgiveness, for MacDonald, is more than the remission of penalties. It is the contact of God’s heart and ours in spite of the wrong between, and the destruction of that wrong. God forgives every day and passes by all he can. His love goes ahead of his forgiveness, and that forgiveness creates ours. But forgiveness needs the human side for its completion, so it cannot reach the one who chooses to stay in his sin. That is the unpardonable thing, and even it is not final. Forgiveness and punishment are not opposed; one may be the condition of the other. To refuse to forgive a brother is spiritual murder, worse than the act of a moment’s passion.
freedomfree · liberty
The condition of a son in his Father’s house. MacDonald defines freedom against both licence and servitude: the man who does as he likes is a slave of sin, and the man who obeys God only from duty or fear is a slave too, though a good one. The only free man is the child who does the Father’s will because he loves it. Freedom comes by the truth, which is Christ, and by dying to self. It is a fullness of bonds of love, not an absence of bonds.
growthgrow · growing
MacDonald thinks of men as growing creatures. God regards them not merely as they are but as they are growing towards the image after which he made them, and so values the thousand imperfect stages of the way. Growth is the more and more life a Christian receives; it comes by obedience and trust, needs strife, and is God’s care rather than ours. Whatever stops it, a final revelation, a cramping creed, distrust, is against the divine order, which is founded for growth.
havingpossession · own · things
The right and the wrong ways of having. Things are given to train a man to “true possession”; held wrongly, they possess him and make him a slave. MacDonald turns the ordinary sense around: only life can possess, only the man who can do without a thing truly has it, and the meek, who claim nothing, inherit the earth. The deepest possession is God himself, the one thing that can be the soul’s own. The opposite principle, “I am my own”, is the principle of hell.
hearthearts · pure in heart
The inmost self, the place where a man’s being springs from God. In MacDonald the heart is not mainly the seat of feeling. It is the deeper self that loves, that is shaped by what it loves, and that knows things the intellect cannot reach. It is also used of God: his heart is the source of all, and the heart in man is made after it. The heart becomes what it clings to, so the pure in heart see God and the heart that haunts earthly treasure rusts with it.
heavenkingdom of heaven · heavenly
MacDonald is impatient with the heaven of popular notion, a place of rest or endless church-going where friends may not know one another. Heaven is first a condition, not a place: continuous touch with God, a loving of all and a forgetting of self, the heaven of the Father’s peace. No spot in space is worth anything to one who remains as he is. The kingdom of heaven is the rule of love and service, entered by becoming a child and hard to enter for anyone who trusts in riches; it comes slowly, by obedience.
hellhell-fire · outer darkness
Hell is real for MacDonald, and terrible, but it is God’s and serves his purpose. It is the condition of a self shut in with itself, away from God, and it is used by God to free his child from sin. It is not endless torment for the sake of punishment, and it is not the devil’s kingdom. He also uses hell for any state of the heart closed against God and its neighbour, here and now.
homeat home · homeward · the Father's house
Home is one of MacDonald’s governing images, and it always points to the Father. Our true home is the Father’s heart, the house we were sent out from so that we might learn it was home; the sinner is called out of the darkness of his exile into the light of his home. Every earthly home, however dear, is only the open porch of the true one, and death is going home to God. Jesus was never away from home, because he was always in his Father’s house, and his face was always set homeward; we are not yet at home in the universe because we are not yet at home with its Lord. A soul is no home until God dwells in it, and he will not force the door. The kingdom of heaven is the home of the children and of perfect brotherhood, and to be at home with God is to be at home everywhere.
hopehoping
The forward lean of faith: expectation, grounded in the Father’s character, of more than we can see or prove. MacDonald makes hope a duty of largeness. Because God is greater than our thoughts, we must hope “to the full extent God has made possible to us”, and a small hope is a small idea of God. Hope reaches beyond death, to the lost, and to the animals and the whole creation. It is not faith, but it is not idle either: it must be let appear.
humanityhuman · inhuman · the humanity of God
Humanity, in MacDonald, means first what man truly is: the essential nature beneath peculiarities and beneath sin, which God made and Christ shows. What is not divine is not essential to it, so the wholly self-made man, savage or sage, is not rightly human, and cruelty and contempt are inhuman. Christ is the blossom of humanity and its ideal, enthroned from eternity, so that the proof of his divinity is his humanity. Behind this lies the humanity of God, in whose image man was made, and whose disbelief explains much irreligion. The word also means mankind as a whole, and a kindly temper.
humilityhumble · meekness · lowly
For MacDonald humility is the child’s way of being, not a low opinion of oneself cultivated for its own sake. The kingdom is entered by becoming a little child, which means ceasing to ask who shall rule and asking who shall serve. Meekness, which he treats at length, is not asserting, defending or avenging oneself; the meek man’s self vanishes in the light of a real presence, and so he alone sees the world as God means it and inherits the earth. True humility comes from seeing God: Job’s self-abhorrence before the face of God was a gift, not a punishment. It grows as a by-product of doing right, while the sense of merit is the meanest form of self-satisfaction. Humility toward one’s brother means looking to him for what of God he alone can show. There is also a false humility: accepting lies on authority, or thinking oneself too bad for God to love.
image of Godlikeness · express image
Man is made in the image of God, and for MacDonald this is the ground of everything else. It is why he is a child and not a machine, why the word of God can reach him, why he cannot live without God, and why God owes himself to his creature. The image may be defaced but never lost: what is spoiled is still God’s image, and that is why it can be evil and miserable. Christ is the express image of the Father, by which we, his imperfect images, learn to read him. The likeness is completed only by his indwelling, and every man is the image of God to every other.
imaginationimagine · fancy
The faculty that gives form to thought, and, in a man, the faculty that finds and reads the forms God has given his thought in the world. It is the human power likest God’s creative act, though it does not create in the strict sense. MacDonald’s essay “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture” is the main source. Fancy is its lesser kin, invention without the laws of meaning.
individualindividuality · apartness
The separate, particular being of each person, which God made deliberately and at cost. MacDonald holds that individuality is not opposed to God or to other people: it is the condition of choosing God freely, the ground of love between persons, and something perfected, not dissolved, in union with God. Each individual has a relation to God that no one else shares, and men are gathered not as a mass but as a body. True individuality comes only through surrender of the self-will; Christ is its generator and perfecter.
inheritanceinherit · heir
The inheritance of the saints is light: everything seen in its derivation from God, so that each saint’s share is a share in the Light. Unlike an earthly inheritance it grows by being shared. Men are heirs because they are God’s children, and the inheritance passes to sons by obedience. The meek inherit the earth because only the meek can see the world as God means it; the grasping owner possesses nothing. See also having · possession.
intellectreason · logic · reasonable
MacDonald does not despise reason. He wants a sufficient reason for what he believes, calls the condition of the gospel absolutely reasonable, and grounds prayer in what is reasonable. But the intellect is not the highest thing in man: intellect, imagination and reason all stand below the will. The mere intellect cannot find out what was born of the heart. The parables, the answer to Job and the Lord’s sayings are addressed to the conscience and the will, not to the logical faculty. To reason from words one does not yet understand is to walk into the mire. Intellectual creeds held against the heart, and a vaunted intellect set against the brother, divide men. The intellect follows obedience; it does not lead.
Jesuselder brother · the Son
For MacDonald, Jesus is the eternal Son of the eternal Father, who made himself what he is by giving himself up wholly to the Father’s will, and so became the source of sonship in all other children. He came not to establish his own power but to reveal the Father, so that men might know God as he knew him. MacDonald’s favourite title for him is elder brother: the first-born of God’s family, who does first what he asks of us, teaches us of the Father whom he alone knows, and has a right to our absolute obedience. He is “divinely human”, the living Christ, never to be replaced by a plan of salvation.
joygladness · the glad creator
Joy is nearer the nature of God than sorrow: God is “the glad creator”, and he made his children to be joyful. Joy is the harmony of the spirit, and its deepest form is the joy of being itself, which is the Father known as Father. Christ’s joy was the Father. Joy is not the reward of selfishness, and the child must be ready to give joy up for truth before he can enter into God’s joy. It comes as the fruit of loving, of obedient labour and of a clear conscience, and it ends every song.
judgmentjudge · condemnation · the judgment of God
MacDonald’s God judges as a father, never as a judge who has put off his fatherhood. No man is condemned for any sin but one, the refusal to leave his sins and come out of them, and the condemnation is of those who see the light and will not come to it. The great judgment is an unmasking: everything covered will be revealed, and the true man welcomes it, even when it shows him wrong. Men are told to love and not judge, since love alone judges a neighbour rightly. Yet we must judge the things of God, humbly and lovingly, and refuse any reading of his words that makes him unlike himself. To be his child is to judge as he judges.
justicejust · fair play
Giving every being what is fair to it, which for God means doing his utmost to make it good. MacDonald separates justice from punishment: punishment may serve justice but does not make it, and the justice of God is the same thing as his mercy. The usual theological sense, God’s need to punish sin or to have it paid for, he treats as a corruption of the word.
kingkingship
One who bears witness to the truth. MacDonald takes Christ’s answer to Pilate, To this end was I born … that I should bear witness unto the truth, as a definition: Jesus is king because he witnesses, and every man who stands for the truth is a king of the same kind. Kingship in this sense is service, and its throne is the cross. The opposite kingship is the self’s claim to be its own king.
knowknowledge
To know, for MacDonald, is above all to be acquainted with a person, as a friend is known, and in the highest case to know God. That knowledge is life itself. Knowledge in the sense of information, facts held in the mind, is real but of a lower order, and set up alone it becomes a rival to life.
lawcommandment · law of liberty
MacDonald uses law of the moral law, of the laws of nature, and of the deepest law of the universe, which is the will of God. The moral law is good but infinite: no one keeps it except by love, and its keeping only shows a hunger it cannot fill. Man is not made for law but for love; love is the creator of law and fills it full. The laws of nature are facts, not yet truths, and are the waving of God’s garments; miracles fulfil the deepest of them. At the end there will be no law but the perfect law of liberty.
lifealive · eternal life
What God is and what he gives, of which all our needs are lack. In the sermon “Life”, on John 10:10, life is the only reality, death a mere negation, and the cure for every ill is “more life”. Real life is self-willed: in God it wills itself, and in man it begins when he wills to live by the life that made him. Eternal life is oneness with God, not endless duration.
lightdarkness · candle of the Lord
First, what God is, on the word of 1 John 1:5; then whatever lets things be seen as they are, from sunlight to the inward light of conscience and reason. MacDonald insists that God’s light is light in our sense of the word, so that nothing which looks to us like darkness can be true of him. Light is also the visible side of life: Christ is light so that men may have life.
loveloving
The nature of God and the law of everything he has made. MacDonald knows “nothing deeper” in God than love, and treats every other attribute, justice, wrath, the consuming fire, as a form of it. In man love is not a feeling to be worked up but the condition he was made for, which God’s love produces in him and which the love of one’s neighbour makes possible.
Makercreation · creature · create
God’s making of beings that are not himself yet like himself, and that can will. For MacDonald creation flows from love: “The being of God is love, therefore creation” (Unspoken Sermons, “Life”). It is the costliest labour of God, begun far back and not yet finished, since the creature is completed only by its own consent. Maker is a true name of God, but not a sufficient one: a God who is only Maker and not Father would make machines, not children. Man’s art may be called creation only in a lesser sense.
mercymerciful
God’s loving dealing with the sinner, and the same disposition in man toward his neighbour. MacDonald’s chief claim is that mercy is not a relaxation of justice but the same thing: God is merciful in rendering to every man according to his work. Mercy is therefore not always gentle; it may punish and compel. And it must pass through a man: he cannot receive what he will not give.
miraclemiracles · wonder-working
A miracle, for MacDonald, is not a breach of nature but a work of the Father done by the Son quickly and in plain view, an epitome of God’s processes in nature seen in immediate connection with their source. The miracles show the will of God at work behind the long gradations of law, hasten what God ordinarily does over years, restore the original order of humanity, and fulfil nature’s deepest laws. Jesus never worked one for himself. Their value lay in revealing the Father’s care to those who could not otherwise see it, and above all in their effect on the people healed.
mysterymysteries · mysterious
For MacDonald a mystery is not the absolutely unknown but what lies behind revelation, the part of a thing that revelation has not yet reached. You must see something before you can feel mystery about it. So mystery and revelation are not opposites: God is always unveiling, and the more He shows, the more there is beyond. A mystery of God that one man has laid hold of is already a revelation, and the mysteries of God are there for His children to look into. Nothing in the Bible is too mysterious to be looked into, and yet the depth of every mystery is God Himself. Some mysteries will stay mysteries for ever, such as self-existence and creation, because no creature could understand them. Mystery belongs as much to persons as to doctrines: to every face, to love between man and woman, to birth and death, and to the common things that Christ turns into radiant mysteries.
namein my name · new name
A true name says what a person is: the character, the nature, the meaning of the one who bears it. Only God can give a man his true name, because only God sees what he was made to be. To act or receive “in the name” of Christ is to act as one who represents him and is like him. God himself is a name only, unless we know him. Because each man’s name is between him and God, there is no room for comparison or ambition, only for aspiration.
naturenatural · Nature
Two main senses. First, the outward world of earth, sky and growth, which MacDonald reads as God’s continuing work and speech; the miracles show in a moment what nature does slowly. Second, the inner constitution of a being: God’s nature, which is love, and man’s nature, whose deepest layer is God in him. The two meet: the truth of nature, outward or inward, is divine.
neighbourneighbourhead · brother
Anyone within reach. MacDonald takes the Good Samaritan as the definition: every one to whom a man can be or do anything is his neighbour. To love him as oneself cannot be done by direct effort of will, nor reached by argument; it comes only from God, in whom alone man meets man. The neighbour is also the test: a man’s relation to God shows in the way he treats the next person, and the merciful toward their neighbour are the ones who can receive mercy.
obedienceobey · obedient
The doing of the will of the Father because it is his will. For MacDonald it is not one virtue among others but the hinge of everything else: the way a man becomes a son, the only way he comes to know God, and the form his faith takes. It is never obedience to men.
onenessharmony · unity · at one
Oneness with God is for MacDonald the end of creation and the content of eternal life and salvation. It is not sameness. God made individuals by separating them from himself so that they might freely become one with him and with each other, and there can be no oneness where there is only one. The pattern and bond of all oneness is the love of the Son for the Father. Harmony is the same thing heard as music: the right relation of every part to the whole, of the creature’s will to the creator’s, of man’s being with itself. Sin is the absence of that harmony; joy is its presence.
opiniondoctrine · theology · notion
What men think and say about God, as against God himself and the doing of his will. MacDonald does not despise thought, but he insists that opinion is not truth and does not save, that the knowledge of Christ is higher than any theology, and that much accepted doctrine, chiefly of adoption, of substitution and of a God with several offices, misrepresents the Father. Theologians, interpreting instead of doing, have hidden the gospel. A right opinion grows out of an obedient life; it cannot produce one.
parableparables · acted parable
For MacDonald the parables are addressed to the conscience and the will, not to the intellect or the imagination. They are strong and direct but not definite, and they are meant not to explain anything but to make a man feel that he is not what he ought to be. That is why they both teach and conceal: they are lanterns of the truth, clear to those who will walk in their light and dark to those who will not, and the ignorance of the unwilling is a kind punishment. The parable is the first form in which truth can be embodied and also the fullest, the form a teacher always comes back to. So MacDonald reads the temptation in the wilderness as a parable Christ told the disciples about His inward experience, fuller as parable than it would be as narrative. Christ’s acts can be parables too: the cleansing of the temple and the withered fig tree are parables in visible form.
poetpoetry · poetic · maker
The poet is the one who sees the truth of things as they are, which is their meaning, and not just the facts about them. Poet means maker, and the poet’s making is the nearest likeness in man to God’s creating, though a gulf divides them. God is the first poet: history is his poetry and the world is his drama. The poet receives what God has uttered in nature and utters it again in human words. Poetry does not stand against science. It includes science, as the face includes the skull. To the man who believes in the Son of God, poetry returns.
polishculture · education · the divine education
For MacDonald, polish does not add anything. It reveals: it takes away whatever comes between the thought and its showing, whether in style, in manners or in the man himself. Its opposite is paint or varnish, ornament stuck on. Applied to the soul, polish is the cutting away of selfishness until the image of God shows through, and its end, like the end of all culture, is simplicity. Education in the true sense is the same work done by God. The whole system of things exists for the education of his children, each lesson given when they are ready for it. The aim of that education is not a balanced repose but a noble unrest.
prayerpray
The child’s asking of the Father, and the beginning of communion with him. In his two sermons on Luke 18:1 MacDonald argues that prayer is natural and reasonable. A God who could not hear would not be God. Our wants exist so that we will come to him, and the answer, which may look like refusal, is shaped by love and may take long. Prayer for others is a real power and a need of love.
punishmentpunish · penalty · retribution
Punishment, for MacDonald, is never an offset to sin, and it makes no atonement for the wrong done. Suffering weighs nothing against sin. God is not bound to punish sin but to destroy it, and he punishes where punishment can help: to make the sinner know what he has done and to soften his heart. So all punishment is kindness. Mere retribution is human, indeed inhuman. The worst punishment is to let a man see what he is, and the moment that sight takes effect he has begun to be righteous. Jesus came to save his people from their sins, not from punishment.
repentancerepent
A change of mind that reaches all the way to will and conduct: the sending away of one’s sins. MacDonald reads metanoia strictly and removes from it the sorrow and shame that usually define it; those may accompany repentance but are not its substance. Repentance is not the price of pardon. God’s forgiveness comes first and causes it; repentance is how the man becomes aware of that forgiveness, and it must be renewed every day.
resurrectionthe body · spiritual body · the redemption of the body
For MacDonald the resurrection of the body does not mean that the same matter rises from the grave; that he calls a puerile notion, against science, common sense and Scripture. It means that God, who is not the God of the dead, will give his children a body again, because the body is the means of revelation both ways: through it God’s world reaches us, and through it we are made known to those who love us. The risen body will be the same body though not the same dead matter, glorified, with all that made each person distinct more visible than before. Christ’s own resurrection he takes as a miracle in the same body, in a glorified state that obeyed the will of the spirit within it. Resurrection is also something to be attained now, the same thing as full sonship: so long as fear or distrust remain in us we have not the life that raised the body of Jesus. The redemption of the body completes the redemption of the spirit; the new man needs a new body and a new earth.
revelationreveal
God’s showing of himself, which MacDonald finds everywhere: in the fire on Sinai, in every fact of nature, in each human soul, in the body, and above all in Jesus, who is the inexhaustible Revelation of God to which the Bible leads. Revelation is always partial, fitted to what the receiver can bear, and it never stops: the Spirit has a revelation for every man. It is not the same as representation, since what is revealed is seen and loved and cannot be refused. Its most terrible form is the revelation of a man to himself.
righteousnessrighteous
Being right in oneself: the fairness of a heart that gives every one his due. MacDonald insists that righteousness is a real condition of character, the same in kind in God and in man, and never a legal status transferred from one person to another. The righteousness “which is of God by faith” (Unspoken Sermons, “Righteousness”) is God’s own righteousness worked out in a man by obedient contact with him. He reserves some of his sharpest language for the doctrine of imputed righteousness.
salvationsave · saved
Deliverance from sin itself, not from its punishment: the making of a man good, a true son of the Father, at one with God. MacDonald attacks the “low” notion of a salvation from consequences as a corruption of the gospel. Salvation is from ourselves, from the possibility of doing wrong again; it is hard, and nothing less than “absolute oneness with God” counts. It cannot be had by correct opinion or a plan, only by faith in the living Christ and the doing of his will, and it is wrought out by us as God works it in us.
seesight · vision · behold
For MacDonald seeing is the highest form of knowing: an inward sight, given to the pure in heart, by which a truth or God himself is known as plainly as a face. It is distinct from proof and from signs, which only produce probability. To see a truth and to love it are one act, and to see God is the end of creation. Sight can be lost to the cares and things of this world, and the will can hold to God even when it cannot see him. The Beatific Vision is the final condition of man; the vision on the way there comes in glimpses.
selfselfishness · self-denial
The conscious “I”, which God gives in order that it may be given back. MacDonald uses the word for two things that must be kept apart: the self that rules and must be denied, and the true self that God made and that appears only when the other is abandoned. God is the “deeper, deepest self” of every person, so denying self is not a loss of identity but the way to it.
sinevil · wrong
Sin is wrongness in a man’s being, not first a list of deeds or a debt owed. At its root is one “master-sin”, the refusal of man’s relation to God, from which all the rest come. Evil is the wider word: the moral wrong in the man and in the world, which God permits for a time and will destroy. Salvation, for MacDonald, is from sin itself and not from its punishment, and the only real remedy is that the sinner be made right.
SpiritHoly Spirit · spirit of the Father
The Spirit is the life of the Father and the Son in a man: the mind of Christ given to him, the spirit of the child toward the Father. MacDonald stresses that the Spirit has a living revelation for each man, which the Bible serves but cannot replace. The Spirit is known by its witness with our spirit, and to shut it out is the one sin that cannot be forgiven while it lasts, since forgiveness can enter only by the Spirit. There is but one spirit, that of truth, and having the same spirit is the only unity.
stupiditystupid · dullness
Not want of intelligence but want of spiritual perception: the dullness that cannot take in what the Lord means. MacDonald finds its cause in care, distrust and the dominion of things, and never in the intellect. The disciples did not understand the warning about leaven because their minds were full of the day’s bread. Stupidity is overcome by trust and by obedience, since the word of the Master unfolds itself only to the man trying to live it. Its worst form cannot know itself.
sufferingpain · sorrow · mourning
Suffering is a means, never an end. It is against the first and last will of God, and it will pass, but it is a real good while it does its work: it brings the wanderer home, burns out selfishness, and opens the heart’s door to God. It is never payment for sin. Punishment weighs nothing against wrong, and to call Christ’s suffering a satisfaction is a pagan idea. God shares our sorrow, though he is not sorrowful in himself, and Christ’s suffering was the greater because his nature was finer. Sorrow is love’s child, and mourning will end in comfort that is more than restoration.
symbolfigure · sign · meaning
All high things can be spoken only in figures, because no thought passes from one mind to another except through the symbolism of creation, which God made so that the seen could speak of the unseen. A true figure never says more than it means. It always says less, and the thing signified lies higher than the sign. So the Lord’s symbols must never be read as meaning less than they say, and they must not be read “after the flesh”, as if they were mechanical. Understanding them depends on having the spiritual fact in oneself. A word that once was a living symbol may wear down into a mere sign.
temptationtempt · tempter
A real pull on the will toward something less than the will of God. MacDonald’s key claim is that the Son of God was tempted not with evil but with good, with inferior forms of good pressing in while the higher waited for God’s time; to yield would have been evil in him. The three temptations in the wilderness are the contest of seen and unseen, outer and inner, show and reality, and each is answered by the principle that the will of God is life. The last and fullest temptation comes in the darkness of the cross. Men must have temptation to rise above it, and must never tempt each other or God.
truthtrue
For MacDonald truth rises through levels. At the bottom is fact, the correctness of a statement. Above that is the truth of a thing, what it is for and what of God it shows. Highest is the truth of a person, a man being what he was made to be in his relations to God and other men. Christ is the Truth in that highest sense, and a man becomes true by doing the truth, not by holding true opinions.
understandunderstanding
The grasp of a thing’s meaning, especially the meaning of God’s words and ways. MacDonald does not despise it: the whole of life is to be “a growth in understanding”. But it follows obedience and cannot replace it, and when a man insists on understanding first he misunderstands. Real understanding comes from within, from likeness to what is understood.
willwills · willed · willing
The power in a person to choose and to act on the choice; in God, the purpose that creates and sustains. For MacDonald it is the highest thing in man, above intellect and imagination, because it is the one place where creature and Creator can meet as two and become one. The human will is God’s gift and yet truly the man’s own. It is free only when it chooses what God wills, and it must not be confused with desire, impulse or inclination, which pass themselves off as will.
wonderwonders · marvel
MacDonald distinguishes the common wonders of God’s ordinary ways from the marvels men crave. The wonder of the growing corn is greater than the miracle of the loaves, and God is more honoured in common wonders than in miracles. A mere marvel is soon forgotten and cannot produce faith; signs and wonders only nourish a faith already there. True wonder, childlike and attendant on imagination, is the seed of knowledge, and the right response to God’s works, to truth once seen, and to the love of the Father and the Son.
workworks · labour · daily work
For MacDonald, work is first God’s: the Father is always working, and Jesus did the works of the Father in little so that men might see his greater works in the corn and the vine. Men are to work as he works. The place of a man’s labour, not the church he attends on his holiday, is the place of divine service, and he is to bear witness in bank, farm, shop and workshop. MacDonald scorns the cry of “the doctrine of works” raised against doing what Jesus said. Faith saves, but a man who would be delivered from evil must himself begin to cast it out. Works done to earn God’s favour are dead works. Daily labour done well is an offering.
worldthe world · worldly · the earth
For MacDonald the world is first the Father’s house, and Jesus was at home in it because he was at home with the Father. We are not yet at home in it, because we are not yet true children, and so we strive with Nature. The earth as God sees it is very good, fit for the meek to inherit, and they inherit it by joy in it, not by holding title to it. The world in the bad sense is the worldly spirit: worldly wisdom that ages a child’s face, the slavish worldly idea of freedom, the world-flattering servant. It is overcome not by fleeing it but by ceasing to believe in outside appearances. Jesus found his symbols in the world, not in the temple.
worshipworth-ship · divine service · in spirit and in truth
For MacDonald worship is the child’s holding up to the Father of what the Father has made him, the turning again of the gift to its source. He hears in the word its old sense of worth-ship, the acknowledging of worth, so that worshipping a God believed to do injustice is worse than believing the lie about him. Worship is graded by what it knows: the worship of fear is true though very low, and God values it as a stage on the way, but only the worship of spirit and truth is acceptable. Each person, having a name known only to God, can worship God as no one else can. Worship is not confined to the temple: the way to worship God by day is to work for one’s fellows. Its counterfeits are the worship of Mammon in churches, self-worship, word-worship and Bible-worship, and the fear of nature that is a kind of worship.
wrathanger · indignation
A form of love. MacDonald insists that God’s anger is real anger, not a figure of speech, and that it is wholly good: “at one with his love, helpful, healing, restoring.” It is directed against sin, never against the sinner as an object of vengeance, and it aims to destroy the sin and deliver the man. He finds it a glad thought that Christ came near enough to be angry with us.
yokeburden · rest
MacDonald’s reading of Take my yoke upon you: the yoke is not one Christ lays on us but the one he wears himself, the will of his Father. To take it is to walk at the other end of it, as oxen walk side by side, drawing with him the load of God’s will, which is the redemption of the world. That yoke is easy, and gives rest from every other weariness. Any other yoke, of a nature contrary to one’s own, of opinions laid on others, or of service the Son never asked for, is slavery.
