A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

parable

parables · acted parable

Frequency in the non-fiction: parable and its forms in 31 paragraphs. The main sources are “The Last Farthing” (third series of Unspoken Sermons), on why the parables both teach and conceal, and “The Temptation in the Wilderness”, where MacDonald reads the temptation itself as a parable told by Christ.

In brief. For MacDonald the parables are addressed to the conscience and the will, not to the intellect or the imagination. They are strong and direct but not definite, and they are meant not to explain anything but to make a man feel that he is not what he ought to be. That is why they both teach and conceal: they are lanterns of the truth, clear to those who will walk in their light and dark to those who will not, and the ignorance of the unwilling is a kind punishment. The parable is the first form in which truth can be embodied and also the fullest, the form a teacher always comes back to. So MacDonald reads the temptation in the wilderness as a parable Christ told the disciples about His inward experience, fuller as parable than it would be as narrative. Christ’s acts can be parables too: the cleansing of the temple and the withered fig tree are parables in visible form.

Shades of meaning

  1. To the conscience, not the intellect. There is a thing wonderful in the parables, “their unintelligibility to the mere intellect. They are addressed to the conscience and not to the intellect, to the will and not to the imagination. They are strong and direct but not definite” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Last Farthing”). See conscience and intellect · reason.

  2. Not to explain but to rouse. “They are not meant to explain anything, but to rouse a man to the feeling” that he is not what he ought to be and does not do what he ought to do (Unspoken Sermons, “The Last Farthing”). The wise give maundering interpretations, “while the child who uses them for the necessity of walking in the one path will constantly receive light from them” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Last Farthing”). The parables of the Truth “are to be understood only by those who walk by what they find” (same). See obedience and understand.

  3. Lanterns of the truth. The parables are plainly for teaching and yet the Lord speaks of them as concealing: “They are for the understanding of that man only who is practical—who does the thing he knows, who seeks to understand vitally” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Last Farthing”). To the unwilling “they should have but parables—lanterns of the truth, clear to those who will walk in their light, dark to those who will not” (same). “Ignorance may be at once a punishment and a kindness” (same). See light and punishment.

  4. The first and the fullest form. “The form of the parable is the first in which truth will admit of being embodied. Nor is this all: it is likewise the fullest; and to the parable will the teacher of the truth ever return” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). As the man becomes real, he “becomes able to understand the words, the symbols, the parables of the Lord” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Cause of Spiritual Stupidity”). See symbol and growth.

  5. The temptation as parable. The inward experiences of Christ “could be conveyed to them only in a parable” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). “Told as a parable, it is as full of meaning as it would be bare if received as a narrative” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). It may not be just as the Lord told it, “and yet may contain in its mirror as much of the truth as we are able to receive” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). Still, “the whole is a faint parable—faint I mean in relation to the grandeur of the reality” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). See temptation and devil · Satan.

  6. Acted parables. Of the fig tree, “This miracle was but the acted parable. Here he puts into visible form that which before he had embodied in words” (The Miracles of Our Lord, “Miracles of Destruction”). The cleansing of the temple was “his first parable to Jerusalem” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Jesus and his Fellow Townsmen”). A saying can be “much like a parable, teaching more than it utters, appealing to the conscience and heart, not to the understanding” (Unspoken Sermons, “Freedom”). See miracle and freedom.

  7. An appeal to common sense. The parable of agreeing with the adversary “is an appeal to the common sense of those that hear it, in regard to every affair of righteousness” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Last Farthing”). Of the good Samaritan: “So direct and complete is this parable of our Lord, that one becomes almost ashamed of further talk about it” (Unspoken Sermons, “Love Thy Neighbour”). See righteousness and neighbour.

In the fiction and poetry. In The Marquis of Lossie Mr Graham, preaching in a London chapel, speaks “a parable to them—of a house and a father and his children” (The Marquis of Lossie, “The Preacher”): children who hide from their father, decide he has gone out, and then that they never had one, while he sits listening for a knock. Lenorme’s painting of Isis is built on a parable of Novalis, which Malcolm reads as Isis “for ever unveiling, that is, revealing herself, in her works” (The Marquis of Lossie, “The Portrait”); see mystery. A group of MacDonald’s poems is titled “Parables” (Poetical Works II, “Parables”).

Neighbouring words

  • symbol (the wider class). Parables are among the symbols a growing man comes to understand.
  • lantern (MacDonald’s figure). Light for those who walk, darkness for those who will not.
  • narrative (the poorer reading). The temptation is fuller as parable than as story.
  • interpretation (the danger). Theologians interpret where they should do.
  • acted parable (the deed form). Miracles that embody in visible form what words had said.

Scripture. Therefore speak I to them in parables: because they seeing see not; and hearing they hear not, neither do they understand (Matthew 13:13). And he spake a parable unto them to this end, that men ought always to pray, and not to faint (Luke 18:1). Agree with thine adversary quickly, whiles thou art in the way with him (Matthew 5:25). Behold, these three years I come seeking fruit on this fig tree, and find none (Luke 13:7).
