A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

wrath

anger · indignation

Frequency in the non-fiction: anger 40; angry 26; wrath 11.

In brief. A form of love. MacDonald insists that God’s anger is real anger, not a figure of speech, and that it is wholly good: “at one with his love, helpful, healing, restoring.” It is directed against sin, never against the sinner as an object of vengeance, and it aims to destroy the sin and deliver the man. He finds it a glad thought that Christ came near enough to be angry with us.

Shades of meaning

  1. Good anger and bad. “There is a good anger and a bad anger. There is a wrath of God, and there is a wrath of man that worketh not the righteousness of God” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Displeasure of Jesus”). Divine anger is the good kind: “God’s anger can be nothing but Godlike, therefore divinely beautiful, at one with his love, helpful, healing, restoring; yet is it verily and truly what we call anger” (same paragraph).

  2. Love shaped by wrong. Anger is one of love’s forms: “Love modelled by faith, is hope; love shaped by wrong, is anger—verily anger, though pure of sin; love invaded by loss, is grief” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Sorrow the Pledge of Joy”). The circle of wrath turns out to be love: “We run within the circle of what men call thy wrath, and find ourselves clasped in the zone of thy love!” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Voice of Job”).

  3. Against the sin, not the sinner. Vengeance is not in God: “If the sinner and the sin in him, are the concrete object of the divine wrath, then indeed there can be no mercy” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”). The wrath consumes the false self to free the true: “The wrath will consume what they call themselves; so that the selves God made shall appear” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Consuming Fire”). See consuming fire.

  4. Never unfair. “Never will his righteous anger make him unfair to us, make him forget that we are dust” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Remission of Sins”). MacDonald rejects any wrath that is “appeased by the suffering of the innocent!” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”). See justice, sense 6.

  5. The anger of Jesus. “The Displeasure of Jesus” studies the times Christ was angry: with the healed leper who disobeyed him, and with the mourners at Lazarus’s tomb, who “wept as those who believe in death, not in life” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Displeasure of Jesus”). “The cloud of his loving anger and disappointed sympathy broke in tears” (same sermon).

  6. A sign of nearness. “It is to me an especially glad thought that the Lord came so near us as to be angry with us” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Displeasure of Jesus”). His anger delivers: “whether he punish directly or indirectly, he is always working to deliver” (same sermon).

  7. Anger in the sons. God’s children are to share it, rightly placed: he will have them “angry where and as he is angry” (Unspoken Sermons, “Abba, Father!”). Human anger is usually impure: “Our anger is in general degrading, because it is in general impure” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Displeasure of Jesus”).

Neighbouring words

  • love (its substance). Senses 1 and 2; see love.
  • consuming fire (its figure). Sense 3; see consuming fire.
  • vengeance, punishment (what it is not). Senses 3 and 4; see justice.
  • terror (how it feels to the impure). See love, sense 2.
  • hate (the root of bad anger). “How different is the anger of one who loves, from that of one who hates!” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Displeasure of Jesus”).
  • grief, sympathy (its companions in Jesus). Sense 5.

Scripture. James 1:20, the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God, quoted in sense 1; John 11:33, he groaned in the spirit, and was troubled (RV margin was moved with indignation in the spirit), the text of “The Displeasure of Jesus”; Mark 1:43, he straitly charged him; Psalm 103:14, he remembereth that we are dust (sense 4); Ephesians 4:26, Be ye angry, and sin not.
