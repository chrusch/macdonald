A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

will

wills · willed · willing

Frequency in the non-fiction: will as a noun after an article or possessive about 340 (the will of God or of the Father 88); the verb forms wills, willed, willing about 170; choose and choice 104.

In brief. The power in a person to choose and to act on the choice; in God, the purpose that creates and sustains. For MacDonald it is the highest thing in man, above intellect and imagination, because it is the one place where creature and Creator can meet as two and become one. The human will is God’s gift and yet truly the man’s own. It is free only when it chooses what God wills, and it must not be confused with desire, impulse or inclination, which pass themselves off as will.

Shades of meaning

  1. The deepest thing in man. The will is ranked above every other power. “For the will is the deepest, the strongest, the divinest thing in man; so, I presume, is it in God, for such we find it in Jesus Christ” (Unspoken Sermons, “Life”). “The highest in man is neither his intellect nor his imagination nor his reason; all are inferior to his will, and indeed, in a grand way, dependent upon it” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Salvation from Sin”).

  2. The place of meeting. Because the human will is distinct from God’s, it can agree with it, and union is agreement, not absorption: “his will must meet God’s—a will distinct from God’s, else were no harmony possible between them” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Salvation from Sin”). In “Life” this is “willed harmony of dual oneness”, and the form it takes is the prayer in the garden: “when he so wills the will of God as to do it, then is he one with God—one, as a true son with a true father” (Unspoken Sermons, “Life”). The same paragraph gives the formula quoted under obedience: “Will is God’s will, obedience is man’s will; the two make one.”

  3. Given, and yet one’s own. The power to will is created; the act of willing is the man’s, and is the share God waits for. “The will, the power of willing, may be created, but the willing is begotten. Because God wills first, man wills also” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). “He gives us the will wherewith to will, and the power to use it … but we ourselves must will the truth” (same paragraph). Hence there is no rivalry between the two wills: “the will of God made the will of man; that, when most it pants for freedom, the will of man is the child of the will of God” (Miracles, “Miracles of Healing Unsolicited”).

  4. The will of God. What God wants, which is always the good of the one he wants it for. Christ’s invitation in “The Creation in Christ” runs: “Together we will do his will, and be glad with him, for his will is the only good” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Creation in Christ”). The vicar of Annals turns it inward: “His will is better than mine, yea, is my hidden and perfected will” (Annals of a Quiet Neighbourhood, ch. XXII, “The Devil in Catherine Weir”).

  5. The real will and its counterfeits. The will proper is born, not given at once, and is set against desire. A Dish of Orts calls its arrival a fourth birth: “the birth in him of the Will—the real Will—not the pseudo-will, which is the mere Desire, swayed of impulse, selfishness, or one of many a miserable motive” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sketch of Individual Development”). Falconer in David Elginbrod draws the line between will and inclination: “When a man is acting with his inclination, his will is in abeyance” (David Elginbrod, ch. XII, “A Sunday with Falconer”).

  6. Free will. Freedom is not choice among indifferent things but the power to do the right. “But a free will is not the liberty to do whatever one likes, but the power of doing whatever one sees ought to be done, even in the very face of otherwise overwhelming impulse” (Miracles, “Miracles of Healing Unsolicited”). Such freedom waits on self-denial: “the will itself is not pure, is not free, until the Self is absolutely denied” (Unspoken Sermons, “Self-Denial”). Falconer again: “The kingdom of heaven is not come, even when God’s will is our law: it is come when God’s will is our will” (David Elginbrod, ch. XII).

  7. Willing to be. The will chooses not only acts but existence, and sonship is its highest choice. “We are not and cannot become true sons without our will willing his will, our doing following his making” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Creation in Christ”). Few yet “respond to the call of God, who will to be, who put forth a divine effort after real existence” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Remission of Sins”). See child, son, sonship.

In the fiction and poetry. Diary of an Old Soul puts senses 2 and 4 into verse: “’Twixt thee and me there’s no division, / Except the meeting of thy will and mine, / The loves that love, the wills that will the same” (Diary of an Old Soul, April), and makes self-surrender the strongest act of will: “To will to die is one with highest life, / The mightiest act that to Will’s hand doth lie” (Diary of an Old Soul, March). Donal Grant gives sense 5 its warning: the man who refuses all obedience “will come by degrees to have no will of his own, and act only from impulse—which may be the will of a devil” (Donal Grant, ch. XXX, “Lord Morven”).

Neighbouring words

  • obedience (its act). Obedience is the human will doing the divine (sense 2). See obedience.
  • choose, choice (its exercise). “Because we are come out of the divine nature, which chooses to be divine, we must choose to be divine” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Creation in Christ”).
  • desire, inclination, impulse (counterfeits). Sense 5.
  • freedom, liberty (its condition when true). Sense 6.
  • self (its rival for the throne). To deny self is “to allow no private communication, no passing influence between the self and the will” (Unspoken Sermons, “Self-Denial”). See self.
  • self-will (its perversion). Rare in the sermons; the vicar of Annals says it outright: “To want one’s own way just and only because it is one’s own way is the height of madness” (Annals of a Quiet Neighbourhood, ch. XIII, “Young Weir”).
  • conscience (its guide). “When the man, listening to his conscience, wills and does the right, irrespective of inclination as of consequence, then is the man free” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sketch of Individual Development”).
  • son, sonship (its goal). Sense 7.

Scripture. Luke 22:42, nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done (KJV), behind sense 2; John 4:34, My meat is to do the will of him that sent me; Matthew 6:10, Thy will be done; John 1:13, born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God, which the Diary echoes: “Shall I be born of God, or of mere man?” (Diary of an Old Soul, February).
