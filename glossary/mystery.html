A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

mystery

mysteries · mysterious

Frequency in the non-fiction: mystery and its forms in 35 paragraphs, many more in the novels. The fullest statement is a conversation in The Marquis of Lossie, chapter XXVIII, “The Portrait”, where Malcolm criticises Lenorme’s painting of Isis.

In brief. For MacDonald a mystery is not the absolutely unknown but what lies behind revelation, the part of a thing that revelation has not yet reached. You must see something before you can feel mystery about it. So mystery and revelation are not opposites: God is always unveiling, and the more He shows, the more there is beyond. A mystery of God that one man has laid hold of is already a revelation, and the mysteries of God are there for His children to look into. Nothing in the Bible is too mysterious to be looked into, and yet the depth of every mystery is God Himself. Some mysteries will stay mysteries for ever, such as self-existence and creation, because no creature could understand them. Mystery belongs as much to persons as to doctrines: to every face, to love between man and woman, to birth and death, and to the common things that Christ turns into radiant mysteries.

Shades of meaning

  1. What lies behind revelation. Malcolm objects that a goddess always veiled has no mystery: “There must be revelation before mystery. I take it that mystery is what lies behind revelation; that which as yet revelation has not reached. You must see something—a part of something, before you can feel any sense of mystery about it. The Isis for ever veiled is the absolutely Unknown, not the Mysterious” (The Marquis of Lossie, “The Portrait”). See revelation and know.

  2. The veil no hand can lift. Because a goddess can never reveal all she is, the painter must show the mystery as well as the beauty: “you must paint the invisible veil that no hand can lift, for there it is, and there it ever will be, though Isis herself raise it from morning to morning” (The Marquis of Lossie, “The Portrait”). She must say without saying it “that she is all there for you, but the all is infinitely more than you can know” (The Marquis of Lossie, “The Portrait”). See art · fairytale and beauty.

  3. A mystery laid hold of. “There is a great difference between a mystery of God that no man understands, and a mystery of God laid hold of, let it be but by one single man. The latter is already a revelation” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Consuming Fire”). God does not want to conceal anything, “but to reveal everything” (same). “The glory of God’s mysteries is—that they are for his children to look into” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Yoke of Jesus”). See child · son · sonship and yoke.

  4. Nothing too mysterious to be looked into. “I cannot admit for a moment that there is anything in the Bible too mysterious to be looked into; for the Bible is a revelation, an unveiling. True, into many things uttered there I can see only a little way. But that little way is the way of life; for the depth of their mystery is God” (Unspoken Sermons, “It Shall Not Be Forgiven”). Those are blessed “to whom a mystery is not a mockery” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Higher Faith”). See understand and faith.

  5. Infinite mystery, given by degrees. Had the face of the Son of God turned on Moses, he would have died “of the actual sight of the incomprehensible” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Consuming Fire”). “If infinite mystery had not slain him, would he not have gone about dazed” (same). “For thus a full revelation would not only be no revelation, but the destruction of all revelation” (same). Even of the temptation in the wilderness, “the mystery is not left behind, nor can the speech be yet clear unto men” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). See see · vision and growth.

  6. The mystery that remains for ever. Self-existence and creation are “a mystery that must be a mystery to us for ever, not because God will not explain it, but because God himself could not make us understand it” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Creation in Christ”). “Self-existence and creation no man will ever understand” (same). See being and Maker · creation.

  7. The mystery of a man. In the face of God “lies the mystery of a man’s own nature, the history of a man’s own being” (The Hope of the Gospel, “God’s Family”). A man bows before “a power to whom he is no mystery as he is to himself” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). No one knows “the mystery whence he sprung by birth, nor the mystery to which he goes by death” (The Miracles of Our Lord, “The Raising of the Dead”). See consciousness and death.

  8. The mystery of love and of persons. Love between man and woman “is one of the most potent mysteries of the living God” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sketch of Individual Development”). “The mystery of individuality and consequent relation is deep as the beginnings of humanity” (Unspoken Sermons, “Love Thy Neighbour”). See love and individual · individuality.

  9. Common things made radiant. At Cana Christ “turns not only water into wine, but common things into radiant mysteries, yea, every meal into a eucharist, and the jaws of the sepulchre into an outgoing gate” (The Miracles of Our Lord, “The Beginning of Miracles”). See miracle and wonder.

In the fiction and poetry. The Portrait conversation is about painting, but its claim that the veil is always being raised and is always there runs through the Lossie books. On the ride home the morning is “A gracious mystery it was—in the air, in the sun, in the earth, in their own hearts” (The Marquis of Lossie, “The Ride Home”), and when Malcolm talks with Clementina about faith, the narrator allows that there may be at work between them “that sacred mystery, holy in the heart of the Father, which draws together the souls of man and woman” (The Marquis of Lossie, “St Ronan’s Well”). In Castle Warlock the narrator turns the word on moral life: “The mystery of all mysteries is the upward tendency of so many souls through so much that clogs and would defile their wings, while so many others seem never even to look up” (Castle Warlock, “A Winter Idyll”).

Neighbouring words

  • revelation and unveiling (what comes first). There must be revelation before mystery.
  • the Unknown (the false mystery). The veil never lifted is not mysterious, only unknown.
  • the incomprehensible (mystery in full). What would slay a man if shown all at once.
  • wonder (the response). Blessed are they to whom a wonder is not a fable.
  • secret (the lesser word). A hidden thing kept back, where a mystery is something partly shown.
  • dark saying (the form it takes in speech). Heard by those with ears to hear.

Scripture. The secret things belong unto the LORD our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever (Deuteronomy 29:29). Unto you it is given to know the mystery of the kingdom of God (Mark 4:11). Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live (Exodus 33:20). For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face (1 Corinthians 13:12). Great is the mystery of godliness: God was manifest in the flesh (1 Timothy 3:16).
