A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

flower

blossom · primrose · rose · daisy · gowan · snowdrop

Frequency in the corpus: flower, blossom and the named flowers in about 90 paragraphs of the non-fiction, chiefly “The Truth” and “The New Name” in Unspoken Sermons and the essays on imagination and on Wordsworth in A Dish of Orts. They are far more frequent in the poems and novels, from which some examples are taken below.

In brief. A flower is God’s thought, sent as a gift of himself to the eyes and hearts of his children. Its truth is not the facts of its botany but the flower itself and what it cannot help saying. Blossom is MacDonald’s favourite figure for the completion of a thing, the end it was made for. So the truth of a thing is its blossom, Christ is the blossom of humanity, and each soul is a distinct flower in God’s garden, named by what it will become. The named flowers each carry their own meaning. The primrose stands for shy, careful trust. The rose is the full summer of Paradise. The daisy, the Scots gowan, is the common flower of childhood and the meek. The snowdrop is the striving, hoping Earth, waiting under the snow for the resurrection.

Shades of meaning

  1. God’s thought, not its botany. “The idea of God is the flower; his idea is not the botany of the flower. Its botany is but a thing of ways and means—of canvas and colour and brush in relation to the picture in the painter’s brain” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). “He thought it; invented its means; sent it, a gift of himself, to the eyes and hearts of his children” (same). The child who finds one “has found one of his silent and motionless brothers, with God’s clothing upon it, God’s thought in its face” (same). See nature and Maker.

  2. The truth of a flower. The man of science will pull it to pieces; the poet will answer, “the flower itself, the perfect flower, and what it cannot help saying to him who has ears to hear it.” “The truth of the flower is, not the facts about it, be they correct as ideal science itself, but the shining, glowing, gladdening, patient thing throned on its stalk—the compeller of smile and tear from child and prophet” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). “Alas for his love and his hope, alas for himself, if the visible should exist for its own sake only!—if the face of a flower means nothing” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sketch of Individual Development”). See truth and beauty.

  3. Blossom as completion. “The truth of a thing, then, is the blossom of it, the thing it is made for, the topmost stone set on with rejoicing” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). A man who has kept every duty to his fellows may still feel, “Like a live flower”, that “he had not yet blossomed, and could not tell what the blossom ought to be” (same). “The Christ in us is our own true nature made blossom in us by the Lord” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Creation in Christ”). The life to come “is one life with the present—as the leaf and the blossom are one life” (Miracles, “The Raising of the Dead”). See growth and humanity, sense 5.

  4. Each a flower in God’s garden. “Each of us is a distinct flower or tree in the spiritual garden of God,— precious, each for his own sake, in the eyes of him who is even now making us” (Unspoken Sermons, “The New Name”), each watered “for the sake of his flower, his completed being, which will blossom out of him at last to the glory and pleasure of the great gardener” (same). “It is the blossom, the perfection, the completion, that determines the name” (same). See name and individuality.

  5. Lilies of the field. The poppies in the corn are “the likest we have to those lilies of the field which spoke to the Saviour himself of the care of God, and rejoiced His eyes with the glory of their God-devised array” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture”). “The little ones may gather their daisies and follow their painted moths; the child of the kingdom may pore upon the lilies of the field, and gather faith” (same). See care · anxiety.

  6. The primrose: shy trust. For the doubter, the time is past when “a primrose might preach a childlike trust; the untoiling lilies might from their field cast seeds of a higher growth into his troubled heart” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sketch of Individual Development”). In David Elginbrod, Sutherland “spied a tiny primrose, peeping out of its rough, careful leaves; and he wondered how, by any metamorphosis, such leaves could pass into such a flower” (David Elginbrod, “The Daisy and the Primrose”). In a poem, the “Content Primroses, / With hearts at rest in your thick leaves’ soft care, / Peeping as from his mother’s lap the child” (Poetical Works I, “Violin Songs”). The primrose is also the promise of the spring: “We know the primrose time will come again” (Poetical Works I, “Within and Without”, Part V, opening sonnet). See faith · trust.

  7. The rose and the snowdrop. “The snowdrop gives birth in our hearts to a loftier spiritual and poetic feeling, than the rose most complete in form, colour, and odour. The rose is of Paradise—the snowdrop is of the striving, hoping, longing Earth” (A Dish of Orts, “Essays on Some of the Forms of Literature”). Neither is above the other: “How shall the rose, the glowing heart of the summer heats, rejoice against the snowdrop risen with hanging head from the white bosom of the snow? Both are God’s thoughts; both are dear to him; both are needful to the completeness of his earth and the revelation of himself” (Unspoken Sermons, “The New Name”). “The snowdrops first—the dawning gray; / Then out the roses burn!” (Poetical Works I, “Violin Songs”). Mourning is “a canker-bitten blossom on the rose-tree of love” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Sorrow the Pledge of Joy”). See suffering · sorrow and hope.

  8. The snowdrop: waiting for resurrection. “Who, after centuries of glory in heaven, would not rejoice to behold once more that patient-headed child of winter and spring, the meek snowdrop?” (Unspoken Sermons, “The God of the Living”). “Secure beneath the earth the snowdrop lies, / Waiting the spring’s young resurrection-day” (Poetical Works I, “Within and Without”, Part III, opening sonnet). “Sad-hearted, be at peace: the snowdrop lies / Buried in sepulchre of ghastly snow” (Poetical Works I, “Violin Songs”). In Annals of a Quiet Neighbourhood the vicar’s ambition is “to brush with gentle hand the earth-stain from the white of one snowdrop” (Annals of a Quiet Neighbourhood, Chapter I). See death and inheritance.

  9. The daisy and the gowan: common joy. Wordsworth’s “The Daisy” shows the poet “playing with the flower” (A Dish of Orts, “Wordsworth’s Poetry”). “Upon the man who can understand the human meaning of the snowdrop, of the primrose, or of the daisy, the life of the earth blossoming into the cosmical flower of a perfect moment will one day seize” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture”). The daisy is the least of lives, and still a true one: “O let me live, if but a daisy’s life!” (Poetical Works I, “Within and Without”). Gowans are daisies in Scots. Cosmo’s hour with his father “spent chiefly amongst the great horse-gowans” was “a portion of the inheritance of the meek” (Castle Warlock, “The Watchmaker”). Old Mr Graham, in London, misses “the laverocks (skylarks), and the gowans” (The Marquis of Lossie, “Mr Graham”). In a Scots song, “the starry gowans wi’ rose-dippit tips / War as white as her cheek and as reid as her lips” (Poetical Works II, “Scots Songs and Ballads”). See child and inheritance.

  10. They die not, though they die. In the poem to the wild flowers, they “rebuke our manhood’s doubt, / And our childhood’s faith renew”, and, coming out of winter, “Tell us that ye only slept— / Think we die not, though we die” (Poetical Works I, “Violin Songs”). They “Pass into the human mind, / Pass with it up into God, / Whence ye came though through the clod” (same). The Lord’s miracles revealed the root, the care of God, “in a few rare and hastened flowers to the eyes that could not see to the root” (Miracles, “The Government of Nature”). See miracle and life.

Neighbouring words

  • root (what the blossom completes). Senses 3 and 10: Love is “at once both the root and the flower of things” (The Hope of the Gospel, “God’s Family”).
  • garden, gardener (God’s care for each). Sense 4.
  • truth (what a flower is). Senses 2 and 3; see truth.
  • botany, science (the ways and means). Senses 1 and 2.
  • spring, winter (the snowdrop’s season). Senses 7 and 8; see hope.
  • heather, harebell, violet (other named flowers). The poems and Scottish novels add the heather of the hills and the harebell, “Whose blue heaven to no wanderer ever closes” (Poetical Works I, “Violin Songs”).

In the fiction and poetry. Partly from general knowledge rather than the corpus searches: flowers fill the fairy tales as well, and in Phantastes Anodos meets fairies who belong to the flowers they live in. Behind many of MacDonald’s gowans stands Burns’s “To a Mountain Daisy”; in Sir Gibbie, Donal has just been reading “the Gowan poem” to Ginevra when he first ventures verses of his own (Sir Gibbie, “The Lorrie Meadow”).

Scripture. Matthew 6:28–30, Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow (sense 5); Song of Solomon 2:1, I am the rose of Sharon, and the lily of the valleys (sense 7); Isaiah 35:1, the desert shall rejoice, and blossom as the rose (sense 7); Isaiah 40:6–8, all the goodliness thereof is as the flower of the field (sense 10); 1 Corinthians 15:36–38, that which thou sowest is not quickened, except it die (senses 8 and 10); Revelation 2:17, the white stone and the new name (sense 4).
