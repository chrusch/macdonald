A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

resurrection

the body · spiritual body · the redemption of the body

Frequency in the corpus: resurrection in about 40 paragraphs, the body in the sense of a person’s body in about 150. The main sources are “The God of the Living” in Unspoken Sermons, chapters VIII, XI and XII of Miracles, and “The Hope of the Universe” in The Hope of the Gospel.

In brief. For MacDonald the resurrection of the body does not mean that the same matter rises from the grave; that he calls a puerile notion, against science, common sense and Scripture. It means that God, who is not the God of the dead, will give his children a body again, because the body is the means of revelation both ways: through it God’s world reaches us, and through it we are made known to those who love us. The risen body will be the same body though not the same dead matter, glorified, with all that made each person distinct more visible than before. Christ’s own resurrection he takes as a miracle in the same body, in a glorified state that obeyed the will of the spirit within it. Resurrection is also something to be attained now, the same thing as full sonship: so long as fear or distrust remain in us we have not the life that raised the body of Jesus. The redemption of the body completes the redemption of the spirit; the new man needs a new body and a new earth.

Shades of meaning

  1. Not the same matter. “Surely we are not required to believe that the same body is raised again. That is against science, common sense, Scripture” (Unspoken Sermons, “The God of the Living”). The material body changes constantly through life, and at death it will be to a man’s consciousness “no more than the old garment he throws aside at night, intending to put on a new and a better in the morning” (same). To insist on its return is like wishing “that the hair which has been shorn from him through all his past life should be restored to his risen and glorified head” (same). See death.

  2. Why a resurrection at all. God cannot be the God of the dead: “To let his creatures die would be to change, to abjure his Godhood, to cease to be that which he had made himself” (Unspoken Sermons, “The God of the Living”). Yet if all live to him, the question remains why they need to rise, and he answers: “For their relation to others of God’s children in mutual revelation; and for fresh revelation of God to all” (same). So the doctrine is “needful as the very breath of life to our longing souls” (same). See life and Father.

  3. The body as the means of revelation to us. “Let us first ask what is the use of this body of ours. It is the means of Revelation to us, the camera in which God’s eternal shows are set forth” (Unspoken Sermons, “The God of the Living”). “It is through the body that we receive all the lessons of passion, of suffering, of love, of beauty, of science” (same), and it “is no less of God’s making than the spirit that is clothed therein” (same). We cannot have learned all that the body has to teach, so the spiritual body of which St Paul speaks “will be a yet higher channel of such revelation” (same). See revelation and nature.

  4. The body as the means of revealing us. “We need not only a body to convey revelation to us, but a body to reveal us to others” (Unspoken Sermons, “The God of the Living”). Therefore “it must be the same body, glorified as we are glorified, with all that was distinctive of each from his fellows more visible than ever before. The accidental, the nonessential, the unrevealing, the incomplete will have vanished” (same). His question is whether this is not “the resurrection of the body? of the same body though not of the same dead matter?” (same). See individual.

  5. Knowing our friends again. He mocks a literalism that keeps the dust and loses the persons: “There are who can believe that the dirt of their bodies will rise the same as it went down to the friendly grave, who yet doubt if they will know their friends when they rise again. And they call that believing in the resurrection!” (Unspoken Sermons, “The God of the Living”). “Shall God be the God of the families of the earth, and shall the love that he has thus created towards father and mother, brother and sister, wife and child, go moaning and longing to all eternity” (same). “No, our God is an unveiling, a revealing God. He will raise you from the dead, that I may behold you” (same). See love and home.

  6. Christ’s resurrection in the same body. Of the Lord himself he writes, “distinctly do I hold that he took again the same body in which he had walked about on the earth, suffered, and yielded unto death” (Miracles, “XI. The Resurrection”), appearing in it to give his disciples “the final consolations of a visible presence, before departing for the sake of a yet higher presence in the spirit of truth” (same). That glorified body “appeared and disappeared at the will of its owner; and it would seem also that other matter yielded and gave it way” (Miracles, “XI. The Resurrection”). The light of the Transfiguration was “a foretaste of his resurrection, a putting on of his new glorified body for a moment while he was yet in the old body” (Miracles, “XII. The Transfiguration”); by the same power “his risen body, half molten in power, was rendered plastic to the will of the indwelling spirit” (Miracles, “XII. The Transfiguration”). See miracle and Jesus.

  7. Resurrection attained as sonship. “So long as there is in us the least taint of distrust, the least lingering of hate or fear, we have not received the sonship; we have not such life in us as raised the body of Jesus; we have not attained to the resurrection of the dead” (Unspoken Sermons, “Abba, Father!”), which in Philippians means, he thinks, the same as the sonship joined to the redemption of the body. Hence his practical question in “Life”: “What have we to do towards the attaining to the resurrection from the dead?” (Unspoken Sermons, “Life”). See child and will.

  8. The redemption of the body. Evil has turned “our house of the body into a prison; rendering it more opaque and heavy and insensible” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Hope of the Universe”). “The redemption of the body, therefore, the making of it for the man a genuine, perfected, responsive house-alive, is essential to the apostle’s notion of a man’s deliverance. The new man must have a new body with a new heaven and earth” (same). “St Paul never thinks of himself as released from body; he desires a perfect one” (same). See freedom and salvation.

  9. Every death ends in resurrection. Jesus let Lazarus die because a hard lesson lay in that death, “the same that lies in every death; and the end the same for all who love—resurrection. The raising of Lazarus is the type of the raising of all the dead” (Miracles, “VIII. The Raising of the Dead”). We have no report from the other side; instead “In Christ we have an ever-growing revelation. He is the resurrection and the life. As we know him we know our future” (Miracles, “VIII. The Raising of the Dead”).

Neighbouring words

  • death (what it answers). Death is throwing aside an old garment; the resurrection is the new one.
  • revelation (the body’s purpose). The body reveals God’s world to us and us to one another.
  • spiritual body (St Paul’s term he adopts). A higher channel of revelation, the same body though not the same matter.
  • redemption of the body (its completion). The perfecting of the greater, the spirit, by the less.
  • sonship (resurrection attained now). Not yet perfected while fear or distrust remain.
  • transfiguration (its foretaste). The light of the risen body shown before death.
  • matter (what does not rise). The particles return to God’s science; the person returns to us.

Scripture. He is not the God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him (Luke 20:38). It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body (1 Corinthians 15:44). If by any means I might attain unto the resurrection of the dead (Philippians 3:11). Waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body (Romans 8:23). I am the resurrection, and the life (John 11:25).
