A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

home

at home · homeward · the Father's house

Frequency in the corpus: home in about 95 paragraphs. The main sources are “Jesus in the World” and “The Heirs of Heaven and Earth” in The Hope of the Gospel, “The Fear of God”, “Light” and “The Cause of Spiritual Stupidity” in Unspoken Sermons, and chapter VIII of Miracles.

In brief. Home is one of MacDonald’s governing images, and it always points to the Father. Our true home is the Father’s heart, the house we were sent out from so that we might learn it was home; the sinner is called out of the darkness of his exile into the light of his home. Every earthly home, however dear, is only the open porch of the true one, and death is going home to God. Jesus was never away from home, because he was always in his Father’s house, and his face was always set homeward; we are not yet at home in the universe because we are not yet at home with its Lord. A soul is no home until God dwells in it, and he will not force the door. The kingdom of heaven is the home of the children and of perfect brotherhood, and to be at home with God is to be at home everywhere.

Shades of meaning

  1. The home of the Father’s heart. “This is and has been the Father’s work from the beginning—to bring us into the home of his heart, where he shares the glories of life with the Living One” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth in Jesus”). The frightened sinner is to run “a true child, for shelter from his own evil and God’s terror, into the salvation of the Father’s arms, the home whence he was sent that he might learn that it was home” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Fear of God”). See Father and salvation.

  2. Out of exile into home. God’s word to the sinner is forgiveness and a summons: “come out of the darkness of your exile; come into the light of your home, of your birthright, and do evil no more” (Unspoken Sermons, “Light”). A man is condemned for not coming to “the living God, who sent the light, his son, into the world to guide him home” (Unspoken Sermons, “Light”). See light and inheritance.

  3. Earthly home as the porch. “Dear as home is, he who loves it best must know that what he calls home is not home, is but a shadow of home, is but the open porch of home, where all the winds of the world rave by turns, and the glowing fire of the true home casts lovely gleams from within” (Miracles, “VIII. The Raising of the Dead”). At the words my Father’s house, “the memory of the poorest home of a fisherman on the shore of the Galilean lake” (Miracles, “VIII. The Raising of the Dead”) would convey more to Peter than any schooling. See symbol and Father.

  4. Death as going home. Why should the mourners at Bethany grudge the elements the body, “because Lazarus was gone home to God, and needed it no more?” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Displeasure of Jesus”). “What matters it that the dead come not back to us, if we go to them?” (Miracles, “VIII. The Raising of the Dead”). See death and resurrection.

  5. Jesus never away from home. In the temple the boy Jesus meant “that he was never away from home, could not be lost, as they had thought him; that he was in his father’s house all the time, where no hurt could come to him” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Jesus in the World”). And whenever he bids us follow him, “he is following the Father; his face is set homeward” (Unspoken Sermons, “Self-Denial”). On the cross he did “in the wild weather of his outlying provinces in the torture of the body of his revelation, which he had done at home in glory and gladness” (Unspoken Sermons, “Third Series”). See Jesus and world.

  6. Not yet at home. “We are not at home in this great universe, our father’s house. We ought to be, and one day we shall be, but we are not yet” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Jesus in the World”). He quotes Novalis on philosophy as home-sickness, “an impulse to be at home everywhere” (A Dish of Orts, “Browning’s ‘Christmas Eve’”), and adds that “The life of a man here, if life it be, and not the vain image of what might be a life, is a continual attempt to find his place” (same). See nature and growth.

  7. No home until God dwells there. God will not force the door. “The terror of God is but the other side of his love; it is love outside the house, that would be inside—love that knows the house is no house, only a place, until it enter—no home, but a tent, until the Eternal dwell there” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Cause of Spiritual Stupidity”). “The only air of the soul, in which it can breathe and live, is the present God and the spirits of the just: that is our heaven, our home, our all-right place” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Cause of Spiritual Stupidity”). The heart is itself a house: “when thou sittest at home in thy house, which is the temple of the Lord, open all thy windows to breathe the air of his approach” (Unspoken Sermons, “Righteousness”). See heart and consuming fire.

  8. The home of the children. The kingdom of God is “the refuge of the oppressed, the golden age of the new world, the real Utopia, the newest yet oldest Atlantis, the home of the children” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Heirs of Heaven and Earth”). “It is the home of perfect brotherhood” (same), and will not open to one who looks down on anyone more miserable than himself. See heaven and neighbour.

  9. The homely duty. The service of God is at home. The divine service is helping “the man who comes near us”; then “Go home and speak to God; nay, hold your tongue, and quietly go to him in the secret recesses of your own heart, and know that God is there” (A Dish of Orts, “True Christian Ministering”). Imagination misused forgets “how to make home blessed or to help the poor neighbour” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture”). With others’ faults, honesty “will end in severity at home and clemency abroad” (Unspoken Sermons, “Love Thine Enemy”). See work and imagination.

In the fiction and poetry. Home and the way home run through the fantasies. In At the Back of the North Wind (1871) the boy Diamond’s going at last to the back of the north wind is his death told as a homecoming, and in Phantastes (1858) Anodos returns from Fairy Land to his own house and his sisters. (General knowledge, not drawn from the corpus.) The pattern is the sermons’ own: the journey out is the way of learning that home was home.

Neighbouring words

  • Father’s house (the scriptural form). The universe is the Father’s house; we are not yet at home in it.
  • exile and far country (its opposite). The sinner’s darkness, out of which he is called.
  • heaven (its fulfilment). Our heaven, our home, our all-right place.
  • family and brotherhood (its members). The kingdom is the home of perfect brotherhood.
  • death (the going home). The porch opens on the true home.
  • heart (the house God would enter). No home, only a tent, until the Eternal dwell there.

Scripture. In my Father’s house are many mansions (John 14:2). Wist ye not that I must be about my Father’s business? (Luke 2:49). I will arise and go to my father (Luke 15:18). If a man love me … we will come unto him, and make our abode with him (John 14:23). While we are at home in the body, we are absent from the Lord (2 Corinthians 5:6).
