A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

neighbour

neighbourhead · brother

Frequency in the non-fiction: neighbour and neighbours 221. The main source is the sermon “Love Thy Neighbour”.

In brief. Anyone within reach. MacDonald takes the Good Samaritan as the definition: every one to whom a man can be or do anything is his neighbour. To love him as oneself cannot be done by direct effort of will, nor reached by argument; it comes only from God, in whom alone man meets man. The neighbour is also the test: a man’s relation to God shows in the way he treats the next person, and the merciful toward their neighbour are the ones who can receive mercy.

Shades of meaning

  1. Whoever is within reach. The Lord taught “that every one to whom he could be or for whom he could do anything was his neighbour, therefore, that each of the race, as he comes within the touch of one tentacle of our nature, is our neighbour” (Unspoken Sermons, “Love Thy Neighbour”). The parable shows “the recognition of kin, of kind, of nighness, of neighbourhood” (same).

  2. Love more than law. The law cannot be kept toward a neighbour without love: “I am certain that it is impossible to keep the law towards one’s neighbour except one loves him” (same). “We are not made for law, but for love” (same). See love and law.

  3. Only through God. Direct effort fails: “The man who will love his neighbour can do so by no immediately operative exercise of the will” (same). “In God alone can man meet man. In him alone the converging lines of existence touch and cross not” (same). See Father.

  4. Not reached by argument. The inner dialogue that asks why one should love one’s neighbour leads nowhere: “no one loves because he sees why, but because he loves” (same). Once a man loves, the old objections “will just be so many arguments for loving” (same).

  5. Otherness the ground. The neighbour’s separateness is not a barrier: individuality “is the sole possibility and very bond of love. Otherness is the essential ground of affection” (same). See self.

  6. The end of human society. “The whole system of divine education as regards the relation of man and man, has for its end that a man should love his neighbour as himself” (same). At last all will be “God-born neighbours”, however debased: “Drink-debased, vice-defeatured, pride-puffed, wealth-bollen, vanity-smeared, they will yet be brothers, yet be sisters, yet be God-born neighbours” (same).

  7. Mercy to the neighbour. The neighbour is where mercy is tested: “It is a small thing to a man whether or not his neighbour be merciful to him; it is life or death to him whether or not he be merciful to his neighbour” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Reward of Obedience”). See mercy, sense 5.

  8. Truth to the neighbour. The smallest duty is first: “May a man become strong in righteousness without learning to speak the truth to his neighbour?” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Cause of Spiritual Stupidity”). See duty and truth.

Neighbouring words

  • love (what is owed him). Senses 2 to 4; see love.
  • law (what love exceeds). Sense 2; see love.
  • brother, sister, kin (his deeper name). Sense 6: neighbours are “God-born”.
  • individuality, otherness (the ground of love). Sense 5.
  • enemy (the hardest neighbour). The next sermon, “Love Thine Enemy”, carries the argument on.
  • mercy, forgiveness (what he must receive from us). Sense 7.
  • duty (the near thing). Sense 8.

Scripture. Leviticus 19:18 and Matthew 22:39, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself, the text of the sermon; Luke 10:29–37, And who is my neighbour?, the Good Samaritan (sense 1); Romans 13:10, love is the fulfilling of the law (sense 2); 1 John 4:20, he that loveth not his brother whom he hath seen, how can he love God whom he hath not seen?
