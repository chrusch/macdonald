A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

art

work of art · artist · fairytale

Frequency in the corpus: art, arts, artist and artistic in about 130 paragraphs, leaving aside “thou art”. Mostly in A Dish of Orts: “The Art of Shakspere”, “St. George’s Day, 1564”, “The Fantastic Imagination” and “On Polish”.

In brief. Art is human making in forms that God has already made. The artist invents no material of his own, so his work always means more than he intended, while God’s work means exactly what he meant. A true work of art does not carry one meaning to be decoded. It wakes meanings in whoever meets it. A fairytale is the freest kind of art: a little world with its own laws, which in physical things may be invented but in moral things must obey. Art is one of God’s good gifts, and nothing a man must forsake to follow Christ, though it may be made an idol for the praise of men.

Shades of meaning

  1. God the first artist. “Let us go further; and, looking at beauty, believe that God is the first of artists; that he has put beauty into nature, knowing how it will affect us, and intending that it should so affect us” (A Dish of Orts, “Wordsworth’s Poetry”). Where a man would make a picture, “God makes the man that makes the book, or the picture, or the machine” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture”). See poet, sense 2.

  2. Borrowed forms. “No artist can have such a claim to the high title of creator, as that he invents for himself the forms, by means of which he produces his new result; and all the forms of man and nature which he modifies and combines to make a new region in his world of art, have their own original life and meaning” (A Dish of Orts, “The Art of Shakspere as Revealed by Himself”). Even so, “in great artists, the amount of conscious art will bear some proportion to the amount of unconscious truth” (same).

  3. It must mean more than its maker meant. “One difference between God’s work and man’s is, that, while God’s work cannot mean more than he meant, man’s must mean more than he meant” (A Dish of Orts, “The Fantastic Imagination”). The reason is that man has only God’s things to use, “his embodied thoughts”, and these carry “layer upon layer of ascending significance” (same). See meaning and symbol.

  4. Waking a meaning. “A genuine work of art must mean many things; the truer its art, the more things it will mean.” A drawing that needs THIS IS A HORSE written under it is no work of art: “It is there not so much to convey a meaning as to wake a meaning” (A Dish of Orts, “The Fantastic Imagination”). “The best thing you can do for your fellow, next to rousing his conscience, is—not to give him things to think about, but to wake things up that are in him” (same). So MacDonald will not explain his tales: “My tales may not be roses, but I will not boil them” (same).

  5. The fairytale and its laws. Man “may, if he pleases, invent a little world of his own, with its own laws; for there is that in him which delights in calling up new forms—which is the nearest, perhaps, he can come to creation” (A Dish of Orts, “The Fantastic Imagination”). But he must keep its laws, and the moral law he may not touch: “In physical things a man may invent; in moral things he must obey—and take their laws with him into his invented world as well” (same). “A fairytale is not an allegory. There may be allegory in it, but it is not an allegory” (same). See law.

  6. Like a sonata, like nature. “The true fairytale is, to my mind, very like the sonata” (A Dish of Orts, “The Fantastic Imagination”); “Nature is mood-engendering, thought-provoking: such ought the sonata, such ought the fairytale to be” (same). And “I do not write for children, but for the childlike, whether of five, or fifty, or seventy-five” (same). See child · childlike.

  7. Reticence and polish. “Shakspere never points to any stroke of his own wit or art. We may find it or not: there it is, and no matter if no one see it!” (A Dish of Orts, “St. George’s Day, 1564”). True polish of style is revelation, not ornament: everything “only pretty and not beautiful” must go (A Dish of Orts, “On Polish”). See self, since the author who obtrudes himself spoils the work.

  8. God’s, not the world’s. “Neither nature, art, science, nor fit society, is of those things a man will lose in forsaking himself: they are God’s, and have no part in the world of evil” (Unspoken Sermons, “Self-Denial”). “The hour is coming when all that art, all that science, all that nature … can afford, shall be the possession, to the endless delight” of the children (same). Yet art can be a treasure on earth, for those “who would make a show in the world by wealth, by taste, by intellect, by power, by art, by genius of any kind” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Heart with the Treasure”). See having · possession and inheritance.

In the fiction and poetry. MacDonald’s own fairytales (The Light Princess, The Golden Key, The Princess and the Goblin) are the practice of sense 5 and 6, and “The Fantastic Imagination” was first written as a preface to an American edition of them. This is general knowledge, not drawn from the corpus.

Neighbouring words

  • imagination, fancy (its faculty). When new forms are “new embodiments of old truths, we call them products of the Imagination; when they are mere inventions, however lovely, I should call them the work of the Fancy” (A Dish of Orts, “The Fantastic Imagination”); see imagination.
  • poet, poetry (its highest form). See poet.
  • law, harmony (its condition). Sense 5; see oneness · harmony.
  • allegory (what it is not). Sense 5.
  • meaning, symbol (what it carries). Senses 3 and 4.
  • ornament, polish (false and true finish). Sense 7.
  • beauty, loveliness (its fruit). See beauty.

Scripture. Genesis 1:31, And God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good (sense 1); Matthew 13:13, because they seeing see not; and hearing they hear not, which bears on the parable as a story that wakes meaning rather than stating it (sense 4); Psalm 104, the Maker’s works (senses 1 and 6).
