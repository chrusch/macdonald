A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

poet

poetry · poetic · maker

Frequency in the corpus: poet, poets, poetry and poetic about 150 paragraphs. Most frequent in A Dish of Orts, above all “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture” and “Wordsworth’s Poetry”, and in the sermons “The Truth” and “The Mirrors of the Lord”.

In brief. The poet is the one who sees the truth of things as they are, which is their meaning, and not just the facts about them. Poet means maker, and the poet’s making is the nearest likeness in man to God’s creating, though a gulf divides them. God is the first poet: history is his poetry and the world is his drama. The poet receives what God has uttered in nature and utters it again in human words. Poetry does not stand against science. It includes science, as the face includes the skull. To the man who believes in the Son of God, poetry returns.

Shades of meaning

  1. Maker. “Poet means maker. We must not forget, however, that between creator and poet lies the one unpassable gulf which distinguishes—far be it from us to say divides—all that is God’s from all that is man’s” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture”). The word belongs to the faculty that “gives form to thought” (same). Later in the same essay MacDonald prefers the Norman name: “Is not the Poet, the Maker, a less suitable name for him than the Trouvère, the Finder? At least, must not the faculty that finds precede the faculty that utters?” (same). See imagination.

  2. God the poet. “Would God give us a drama? He makes a Shakespere.” When he would have lyrics, “he sets his birds and his maidens a-singing. All the processes of the ages are God’s science; all the flow of history is his poetry” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture”). Hence the essay’s last sentence: “he that will do the will of THE POET, shall behold the Beautiful” (same). See Maker · creation.

  3. The truth of a thing. “Ask the poet what is the truth of the flower, and he will answer: ‘Why, the flower itself, the perfect flower, and what it cannot help saying to him who has ears to hear it.’” The man of science laughs at this, “but the poet and the child care as little for his laughter as the birds of God” care for “his treatise on aerostation” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). This is “a region in which the poet is among his own things, and to which he has often to go to fetch them” (same). See truth.

  4. The outer show. “The poet deals with the outer show of things, which outer show is infinitely deeper in its relation to truth, as well as more practically useful, than the analysis of the man of science” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Mirrors of the Lord”). St. Paul’s mirror is “that of a poet, not a man of science” (same): a glass that takes the face into itself. See see · sight.

  5. Poetry includes science. “Nature put into the crucible of a loving heart becomes poetry. We cannot explain poetry scientifically; because poetry is something beyond science.” Indeed “poetry includes science, and the man who will advance science most, is the man who, other qualifications being equal, has most of the poetic faculty in him” (A Dish of Orts, “Wordsworth’s Poetry”). In the life of the growing man, “Poetry is true as Science, and Science is holy as Poetry; but young Poetry is timid and Science is fearless” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sketch of Individual Development”). Later he “begins to descry the indwelling poetry of science” (same).

  6. Uttering again what God uttered. In Wordsworth “nature passes into poetry; that form is sublimed into speech.” “The outflowing of spirit in nature is received by the poet, and he utters again, in his form, what God has already uttered in His” (A Dish of Orts, “Wordsworth’s Poetry”). All words for the inner world “are of the imagination, are originally poetic words”, and lose that aspect with use: “It ceases to be heard as a symbol, and appears only as a sign” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture”). See nature and revelation.

  7. More alive. “The sage is a larger life than the clown; the poet is more alive than the man whose life flows out that money may come in” (Unspoken Sermons, “Life”), though the man to whom God is all in all is more alive still. “No poet of high rank, as far as I know, ever disbelieved in the future. He might fear that there was none; but that very fear is faith” (Miracles, “The Raising of the Dead”). See life.

  8. Poetry returns. “To the man who believes in the Son of God, poetry returns in a mighty wave; history unrolls itself in harmony; science shows crowned with its own aureole of holiness” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sketch of Individual Development”). Without that faith, even “the written poetry of his fellows” can give no rest, “for it is not any of the forms, even of the thought-forms of truth that can give rest to his soul” (same). In the dark hour of “The Eloi”, “art, poetry, religion are nothing” to the sufferer (Unspoken Sermons, “The Eloi”).

In the fiction and poetry. MacDonald was a poet first. His first major published work was the dramatic poem Within and Without, and his best-known verse, A Book of Strife in the Form of the Diary of an Old Soul, is a year of daily prayer-stanzas. In Phantastes Anodos frees the marble lady by singing to her, a picture of the poet whose song calls a hidden life into the open. These notes come from general knowledge, not from the corpus.

Neighbouring words

  • imagination, fancy (the faculty). Senses 1 and 6; see imagination.
  • maker, finder, trouvère (the old names). Sense 1.
  • science, man of science (the contrast). Senses 3 to 5; poetry includes science and goes beyond it.
  • prophet (its twin). Paul was “poet or prophet” and “was both” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Mirrors of the Lord”).
  • child (its companion). Sense 3; see child · childlike.
  • art, artist (its craft). See art · work of art · fairytale.
  • beauty (its end). Poetry is “truth in beauty” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture”); see beauty.

Scripture. Psalm 19:1, The heavens declare the glory of God (sense 2); 2 Corinthians 3:18, beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord (sense 4); Acts 17:28, as certain also of your own poets have said, For we are also his offspring, which MacDonald sets beside the Old Testament poets’ “work of his hand” (A Dish of Orts, “Wordsworth’s Poetry”); Matthew 6:28–29, Consider the lilies of the field (sense 3).
