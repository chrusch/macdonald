A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

humility

humble · meekness · lowly

Frequency in the corpus: humble, humility, meek and lowly in about 90 paragraphs. The main sources are “The Heirs of Heaven and Earth”, “The Reward of Obedience” and “The Yoke of Jesus” in The Hope of the Gospel, with “The Child in the Midst”, “The Voice of Job” and “The New Name” in Unspoken Sermons.

In brief. For MacDonald humility is the child’s way of being, not a low opinion of oneself cultivated for its own sake. The kingdom is entered by becoming a little child, which means ceasing to ask who shall rule and asking who shall serve. Meekness, which he treats at length, is not asserting, defending or avenging oneself; the meek man’s self vanishes in the light of a real presence, and so he alone sees the world as God means it and inherits the earth. True humility comes from seeing God: Job’s self-abhorrence before the face of God was a gift, not a punishment. It grows as a by-product of doing right, while the sense of merit is the meanest form of self-satisfaction. Humility toward one’s brother means looking to him for what of God he alone can show. There is also a false humility: accepting lies on authority, or thinking oneself too bad for God to love.

Shades of meaning

  1. Humbling oneself as a child. The disciples disputed who should be greatest, and Jesus set a child among them: “He told them they could not enter into the kingdom save by becoming little children—by humbling themselves. For the idea of ruling was excluded where childlikeness was the one essential quality. It was to be no more who should rule, but who should serve” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Child in the Midst”). In this kingdom the spirit of children runs throughout, “from lowly subject to lowliest king” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Child in the Midst”). See child and king.

  2. Meekness: not asserting oneself. “The meek are those that do not assert themselves, do not defend themselves, never dream of avenging themselves, or of returning aught but good for evil. They do not imagine it their business to take care of themselves” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Heirs of Heaven and Earth”). “The meek man’s self always vanishes in the light of a real presence” (same), and no bristling importance guards his door. See self and neighbour.

  3. The meek inherit the earth. “We cannot see the world as God means it, save in proportion as our souls are meek. In meekness only are we its inheritors. Meekness alone makes the spiritual retina pure to receive God’s things as they are” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Heirs of Heaven and Earth”). Gladly would the men of Nazareth have inherited the earth, “but they were not meek, and the earth was for the lowly children of the perfect Father” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Jesus and his Fellow Townsmen”). See inheritance, world and having.

  4. Meek and lowly toward the Father. He paraphrases Christ’s invitation: “Be meek and lowly of heart toward him as I am; let him lay his yoke upon you as he lays it on me. I do his will, not my own” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Yoke of Jesus”). Christ knows only one way to free men from their sins: “he will teach them to be like himself, meek and lowly, bearing with gladness the yoke of his father’s will” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Salvation from Sin”). See yoke and obedience.

  5. Humility from seeing God. Job “saw the face of God—and abhorred himself in dust and ashes. He sought justification; he found self-abhorrence. Was this punishment? The farthest from it possible” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Voice of Job”). “Oh the love of the Father, that he should give us to compare ourselves with him, and be buried in humility and shame! To be rebuked before him is to be his” (same). The final unmasking may likewise redound to the glory of the truth “by making you ashamed and humble” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Final Unmasking”). See see and judgment.

  6. Humbler by doing right. Humility is not pursued but grows: “God’s reward lies closed in all well-doing: the doer of right grows better and humbler, and comes nearer to God’s heart as nearer to his likeness” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Reward of Obedience”). Its enemy is merit: “a sense of merit is the most sneaking shape that self-satisfaction can assume” (same). “To be made greater than one’s fellows is the offered reward of hell, and involves no greatness; to be made greater than one’s self, is the divine reward” (same). See righteousness and growth.

  7. Humble before my brother. Since each person knows God as no one else can, “God give me grace to be humble before thee, my brother, that I drag not my simulacrum of thee before the judgment-seat of the unjust judge, but look up to thyself for what revelation of God thou and no one else canst give” (Unspoken Sermons, “The New Name”). Seeing the traitor’s germ in ourselves should make us gentle: “Let us therefore be compassionate and humble, and hope for every man” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Final Unmasking”). See name and neighbour.

  8. Humble before the truth. “Above all, let us be humble before the God of truth, faithfully desiring of him that truth in the inward parts which alone can enable us to walk according to that which we have attained” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sermon”). The opinion a man worships is the one he must change: “Let it humble thee to know that thy dearest opinion, the one thou dost worship as if it, and not God, were thy Saviour, this very opinion thou art doomed to change” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sermon”). The seeker who comes “in humble readiness to accept him altogether if only he can” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Jesus and his Fellow Townsmen”) will find Christ. See truth, opinion and doubt.

  9. The humble imagination. In the life of the mind too, he claims “for the true, childlike, humble imagination, such an inward oneness with the laws of the universe that it possesses in itself an insight into the very nature of things” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture”). The man of science, rapt in the grandeur of what he beholds, “no more thinks of himself, but in humility is great, and knows it not” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sketch of Individual Development”). See imagination.

  10. False humility. Good men received the hard doctrines of the schools “in their humility as recognized truths, instead of inventions of men” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”), and did not dare dispute them. Of a man in depression he writes, “If he is still humble, he thinks that he is so bad that God cannot care for him” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Eloi”), believing God loves us only while we love him. Neither is the humility of a child. See Father and doubt.

Neighbouring words

  • childlikeness (its root). Humility is the child’s way, not self-abasement.
  • meekness (its outward form). Not asserting, defending or avenging oneself.
  • self-denial (its discipline). The self vanishes in a real presence.
  • merit (its enemy). The most sneaking shape of self-satisfaction.
  • service (its work). Not who shall rule but who shall serve.
  • shame and self-abhorrence (its first experience before God). A gift, not a punishment.
  • pride and self-importance (its opposites). The bristling importance that guards the door.

Scripture. Whosoever therefore shall humble himself as this little child, the same is greatest in the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 18:4). Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth (Matthew 5:5). Learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart (Matthew 11:29). Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes (Job 42:6). When ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants (Luke 17:10).
