A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

imagination

imagine · fancy

Frequency in the non-fiction: imagination and imaginations 356; fancy 55.

In brief. The faculty that gives form to thought, and, in a man, the faculty that finds and reads the forms God has given his thought in the world. It is the human power likest God’s creative act, though it does not create in the strict sense. MacDonald’s essay “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture” is the main source. Fancy is its lesser kin, invention without the laws of meaning.

Shades of meaning

  1. The faculty that gives form to thought. The definition: “The imagination is that faculty which gives form to thought—not necessarily uttered form, but form capable of being uttered in shape or in sound, or in any mode upon which the senses can lay hold” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination: Its Functions and its Culture”).

  2. God’s imagination, and creation. The divine imagination is the source of all things: “It is better to keep the word creation for that calling out of nothing which is the imagination of God” (same essay). In the sermons: “His imagination is one with his creative will. The thing that God imagines, that thing exists” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Creation in Christ”). Water “comes bubbling fresh from the imagination of the living God” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”).

  3. Man’s imagination: made in the image of God’s. Man’s imagination works with forms already given, but works as God’s does: “its operation is the same as that of the divine inasmuch as it does put thought into form” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination”). Hence the poet: “Poet means maker” (same essay).

  4. Finding out the divine imagination. Man’s first duty with the faculty is not making but reading: “that of following and finding out the divine imagination in whose image it was made” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination”). So “To inquire into what God has made is the main function of the imagination. It is aroused by facts, is nourished by facts” (same essay).

  5. The prophetic or discovering imagination. Imagination goes ahead of science and guesses the law before the intellect proves it: “We yield you your facts. The laws we claim for the prophetic imagination” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination”). In the sermons, the “poetic imagination divinely alive, whose part is to foresee and welcome approaching truth; to discover the same principle in things that look unlike” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Mirrors of the Lord”).

  6. The imagination that recognizes truth. In “The Truth”, the imagination is the faculty that sees a thing’s meaning: “truth in a man’s imagination is the power to recognize this truth of a thing” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). God’s answer to Job speaks “to the revealing, God-like imagination in the man, and to no logical faculty whatever” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Voice of Job”).

  7. The religious imagination, holy and unholy. A good imagination is needed to think rightly of God and heaven; its absence produces dismal doctrines. Children fear heaven because of what is told them by those “without imagination” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Inheritance”); the legal theory of imputed righteousness is “the meagre misshapen offspring of the legalism of a poverty-stricken mechanical fancy, unlighted by a gleam of divine imagination” (Unspoken Sermons, “Righteousness”). The plural imaginations is usually the bad sense, the shapes made by fear or lust: “misery will beget on imagination a thousand shapes of woe” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Last Farthing”).

  8. Below the will. For all its dignity, imagination is not the highest thing in man: “The highest in man is neither his intellect nor his imagination nor his reason; all are inferior to his will” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Salvation from Sin”). The parables are “addressed to the conscience and not to the intellect, to the will and not to the imagination” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Last Farthing”).

  9. Meaning in the reader. In “The Fantastic Imagination” the reader’s imagination finds its own meaning, and that is no failure: “A genuine work of art must mean many things; the truer its art, the more things it will mean” (A Dish of Orts, “The Fantastic Imagination”).

Fancy. Fancy is the faculty of resemblances without law or interpretation. The teacher “will teach him not to mistake fancy, either in himself or in others for imagination, and to beware of hunting after resemblances that carry with them no interpretation” (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination”). Yet fancy has laws of its own, and the child must not “sin against fancy’s sense; for fancy has its laws as certainly as the most ordinary business of life” (same essay). Fancy can serve imagination: “fancy itself will subserve the true imagination of the mystic, and so be glorified” (Unspoken Sermons, “The New Name”).

In the fiction. Donal Grant draws the line between imagining and believing: “The imagination is an endless help towards faith, but it is no more faith than a dream of food will make us strong for the next day’s work” (Donal Grant, ch. I, “Foot-Faring”).

Neighbouring words

  • form, thought (its matter and its product). Sense 1.
  • create, make, maker, poet (the act it resembles). Senses 2 and 3.
  • fact (its nourishment, not its rival). Sense 4.
  • intellect, understanding (companion faculties). The intellect follows and proves; the imagination foresees (sense 5). “He hath set the world in man’s heart,” not in his understanding (A Dish of Orts, “The Imagination”).
  • truth (its object). Sense 6 and truth.
  • will, faith (its superiors). Sense 8 and the Donal Grant passage.
  • fancy (its lesser kin). See above.
  • meaning, symbol (what it reads and makes). Sense 9.

Scripture. Ecclesiastes 3:11, he hath set the world in their heart (KJV), which MacDonald quotes for sense 5; Genesis 1:27, in the image of God created he him, behind sense 3.
