A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

doubt

doubts · doubting · honest doubt

Frequency in the corpus: doubt and doubting in about 120 paragraphs of the non-fiction, many in the idiom “no doubt”. The main sources are “The Voice of Job” in Unspoken Sermons, “A Sketch of Individual Development” in A Dish of Orts and “Jesus and his Fellow Townsmen” in The Hope of the Gospel.

In brief. MacDonald does not treat doubt as the enemy of faith. Doubts are the messengers of the Living One, sent to rouse the honest; they are the first knock of truths not yet understood, and every deeper assurance must be preceded by them. A man may be haunted by doubts and grow by them in faith. What matters is the will behind the doubt. Childlike doubt, ready to believe if it can, is dear to God and will feed the faith at its heart; the doubt of a man who would only use certainty to go on disobeying receives no answer. The worst doubt is not of God’s existence but of his goodness, and doubt is answered not by argument but by obedience and the sight of Jesus.

Shades of meaning

  1. Messengers of the Living One. “A man may be haunted with doubts, and only grow thereby in faith. Doubts are the messengers of the Living One to rouse the honest. They are the first knock at our door of things that are not yet, but have to be, understood; and theirs in general is the inhospitable reception of angels that do not come in their own likeness” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Voice of Job”). “Doubt must precede every deeper assurance; for uncertainties are what we first see when we look into a region hitherto unknown, unexplored, unannexed” (same). See faith and growth.

  2. Doubt of goodness worse than doubt of being. In Job’s confusion there may have mingled “some element of doubt as to the existence of God. Let not such doubt be supposed a yet further stage in unbelief” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Voice of Job”). To deny God’s existence may “involve less unbelief than the smallest yielding to doubt of his goodness” (same). When God answers out of the whirlwind, Job needs no explanation: “he knows that God is, and that he hears the cry of his creature” (same). See being and Father.

  3. Childlike doubt and wise unbelief. “Let any man seek him—not in curious inquiry whether the story of him may be true or cannot be true—in humble readiness to accept him altogether if only he can, and he shall find him; we shall not fail of help to believe because we doubt” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Jesus and his Fellow Townsmen”). But if the dispersion of a man’s doubt “would but leave him in disobedience, the Power of truth has no care to effect his conviction” (same). “The childlike doubt will, as it softens and yields, minister nourishment with all that was good in it to the faith-germ at its heart; the wise and prudent unbelief will be left to develop its own misery” (same). See child and obedience.

  4. Doubts on the way to Christ. In MacDonald’s sketch of a soul’s growth, the seeker is beset by “endless doubts and questions. What if the whole idea of his mission was a deception born of the very goodness of the man?” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sketch of Individual Development”). He gathers evidence and argument and “finds himself much where he was before. But at least he is haunting the possible borders of discovery” (A Dish of Orts, “A Sketch of Individual Development”). What settles it is the discovery “that only the obedient, childlike soul can understand” the words of Jesus (A Dish of Orts, “A Sketch of Individual Development”). See understand and intellect.

  5. Doubting what we were taught. Some find it hard to forsake “such notions of God and his Christ as they were taught in their youth”, notions “of which they have begun to doubt the truth, but to cast which away seems like parting with every assurance of safety” (Unspoken Sermons, “Self-Denial”). MacDonald encourages that doubt. We are bound to refuse any reading that is unworthy of Christ, “to refuse and wait for more light” (Unspoken Sermons, “It Shall Not be Forgiven”). See opinion and judgment.

  6. Bring your doubts to him. The answer to doubt is not to hide it: “come to him with all thy doubts, fears, dishonesties, meannesses, paltrinesses, misjudgments, wearinesses, disappointments, and stalenesses: be sure he will take thee” (Unspoken Sermons, “Light”). Christ in his victory “had overcome the cause of all doubt, the belief in the outside appearances and not in the living truth” (Miracles, “The Raising of the Dead”). And “because his humanity is not comprehended, his divinity is doubted” (A Dish of Orts, “Browning’s Christmas Eve”). See Jesus and humanity.

In the fiction and poetry. In The Annals of a Quiet Neighbourhood the vicar says of a doubting man: “A man may be on the way to the truth, just in virtue of his doubting” (Annals of a Quiet Neighbourhood, ch. IV, “The Coffin”), and he takes the man’s real, unaffected doubt as much in his favour. (General knowledge, not drawn from the corpus:) Robert Falconer traces a boy’s doubts of his grandmother’s Calvinism through to a deeper faith.

Neighbouring words

  • unbelief (the opposite of doubt). The refusal of a will that does not want truth.
  • faith · trust (goal). Doubt honestly followed grows into it.
  • questioning · inquiry (method). Useful, but secondary to obedience.
  • uncertainty (first sight). What we see first in any unexplored region of truth.

Scripture. Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief (Mark 9:24). Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him (Job 13:15). O thou of little faith, wherefore didst thou doubt? (Matthew 14:31).
