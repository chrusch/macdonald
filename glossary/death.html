A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

death

die · dead

Frequency in the non-fiction: death 303; die 102; dead 230.

In brief. A negation, not a power. MacDonald’s death has two sides. Bodily death is a passing, a release and a renewal of ties, which the God of the living cannot let be final. The real death is the self-centred life cut off from God, and the way out of it is another dying, the death to self that Christ died. The opposite of death is not survival but life, and both words are best read with life.

Shades of meaning

  1. A negation. “Life is the only reality; what men call death is but a shadow—a word for that which cannot be—a negation, owing the very idea of itself to that which it would deny” (Unspoken Sermons, “Life”). “God is being; death is nowhere!” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Fear of God”). See life, sense 3.

  2. Not final: the God of the living. God cannot let his creatures go: “To let his creatures die would be to change, to abjure his Godhood, to cease to be that which he had made himself” (Unspoken Sermons, “The God of the Living”). Death does not cut love off: “Death is not an impervious wall; through it, beyond it, go the prayers” (Unspoken Sermons, “Man’s Difficulty Concerning Prayer”).

  3. Dying to self. The good death, and the one MacDonald urges. “If we do not die to ourselves, we cannot live to God, and he that does not live to God, is dead” (Unspoken Sermons, “Freedom”). See self and life, sense 7.

  4. The death of the self-centred. The man who lived for himself is already in a dead world: “In the midst of the live world he cared for nothing but himself; now in the dead world he is in God’s prison, his own separated self” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Last Farthing”).

  5. The corruption to be burned away. Death is also what clings to the soul and must be consumed: the glory of God, “shining upon the corrupt and deathly, must needs, like the sun, consume the dead” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Fear of God”); hell exists “to free the child of God from the corruption of death” (The Hope of the Gospel, “Salvation from Sin”). See consuming fire.

  6. Bodily death as release. In the novels death is a friend that frees and joins. “Death is not a breaker but a renewer of ties” (Alec Forbes of Howglen, ch. LXXXVII). “Age is in fact the mason that builds up the doors and the windows, and death is the angel that breaks the prison-house and lets the captives free” (The Seaboard Parish, ch. VI).

In the fiction and poetry. Lilith turns the words round: the narrator will see true and false clearly only “when you are quite dead—that is, quite alive” (Lilith, ch. XLIII, “The Dreams That Came”). In the Diary death brings the soul nearer: “He comes—and goes—to leave me in thy arms, / Nearer thy heart, oh, nearer than before!” (Diary of an Old Soul, January).

Neighbouring words

  • life (the reality it negates). Sense 1; see life.
  • self, selfishness (what dies; what kills). Senses 3 and 4; see self.
  • resurrection (its undoing). Sense 2.
  • corruption (its substance). Sense 5.
  • hell, outer darkness, prison (its spiritual form). Sense 4.
  • sleep (its figure in the novels). “If death is not a sleep, Jesus did not speak the truth when he said Lazarus slept” (The Seaboard Parish, ch. X, “The Sermon”).

Scripture. Luke 20:38, he is not a God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him, the text of “The God of the Living”; John 12:24, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die; Romans 6:11, dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto God (sense 3); John 11:11, Our friend Lazarus sleepeth.
