A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

consuming fire

fire of God

Frequency in the non-fiction: consuming fire 22, fire and fires 138, burn and its forms 75. Most of the doctrine is in the sermon “The Consuming Fire” and in “The Fear of God”.

In brief. MacDonald’s name for the love of God as it meets what is impure, taken from Hebrews 12:29. The fire is not a second thing in God beside his love; it is the love itself, which destroys sin and spares the sinner. It is felt as torment by those who hold on to their sin and as light or comfort by those who come near.

Shades of meaning

  1. Love itself. The fire is God’s nature, not his anger set against his love: “The consuming fire is just the original, the active form of Purity,—that which makes pure, that which is indeed Love, the creative energy of God” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Consuming Fire”). “The fire of God, which is his essential being, his love, his creative power” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Fear of God”).

  2. Inexorable. Because it is love it does not stop. “Escape is hopeless. For Love is inexorable. Our God is a consuming fire” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Consuming Fire”). The sinner may fear it, but “Can it be any comfort to them to be told that God loves them so that he will burn them clean” (same sermon).

  3. What it consumes. The fire takes the false self and leaves the true one. “The wrath will consume what they call themselves; so that the selves God made shall appear” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Consuming Fire”). “Many a man’s work must be burned, that by that very burning he may be saved” (same sermon).

  4. Hot only at a distance. The fire burns in proportion to the distance from God: “it is only at a distance it burns—that the farther from him, it burns the worse, and that when we turn and begin to approach him, the burning begins to change to comfort” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Fear of God”). So “Justice” speaks of “the consuming fire of his distance” and of the life-giving fire “whose outer circles burn” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”).

  5. The outer darkness. Hell is the same fire with the light withdrawn: “the outer darkness is but the most dreadful form of the consuming fire—the fire without light—the darkness visible, the black flame” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Consuming Fire”). Even there “he keeps him alive by his fire” (same paragraph).

  6. Courted by the good. For the one who loves God the fire is welcome, and may be felt as light. “The man who loves God, and is not yet pure, courts the burning of God. Nor is it always torture. The fire shows itself sometimes only as light” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Consuming Fire”). The sermon ends in prayer: “And thou wilt not burn us more than we can bear. But thou wilt burn us” (same sermon).

  7. The fire that ends hell. Last of all the fire consumes death and hell themselves: “But at length, O God, wilt thou not cast Death and Hell into the lake of Fire—even into thine own consuming self?” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Consuming Fire”).

In the fiction and poetry. The novels use the phrase of love wherever it meets wrong. In Alec Forbes the crippled Truffey follows the schoolmaster who maimed him, “heaping on his head the coals of that consuming fire which is love, which is our God” (Alec Forbes of Howglen, ch. LVI); in Heather and Snow, “True love is an awful thing, not to the untrue only, but sometimes to the growing-true, for to everything that can be burned it is a consuming fire” (Heather and Snow, ch. XXXVII, “A Great Gulf”). The fairy tales give it an image: Curdie, told to, “thrust both of his hands right into the middle of the heap of flaming roses”, and “it did hurt! But he did not draw them back” (The Princess and Curdie, ch. 8, “Curdie’s Mission”). The Diary takes sense 6 as a vow: “gently in thy fire / I will lie burning” (Diary of an Old Soul, October).

Neighbouring words

  • love (identity). Sense 1.
  • purity, purify (its work). Sense 1; “the purifying that dwells therein” (Unspoken Sermons, “It Shall Not be Forgiven”).
  • fear (its first effect). “The Fear of God” is the companion sermon; fear lasts until the fire is known as love (sense 4).
  • wrath (its appearance to the sinner). Sense 3.
  • punishment, hell, outer darkness (its forms). Sense 5; see justice.
  • light (its other face). Sense 6; see light.
  • baptism of fire (its gift). The Baptist’s word is read as power to put sin away: “the man’s one preparation for receiving the power to overcome them, the baptism of fire” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Remission of Sins”).

Scripture. Hebrews 12:29, For our God is a consuming fire, with verses 27–28 on the things which cannot be shaken; Deuteronomy 4:24; 1 Corinthians 3:15, he himself shall be saved; yet so as by fire (sense 3); Matthew 5:26, till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing; Revelation 20:14, death and hell were cast into the lake of fire (sense 7); Mark 9:49, every one shall be salted with fire, the title text of Salted with Fire.
