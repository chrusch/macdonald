A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

atonement

at-one-ment · sacrifice · vicarious sacrifice

Frequency in the corpus: atonement in about 20 paragraphs, sacrifice and sacrificing in about 35, almost all in Unspoken Sermons. The main source is “Justice” in the third series, with “Life”, “The Hands of the Father” and “Righteousness”.

In brief. MacDonald refuses the atonement as his questioner means it, a satisfaction paid to God’s justice by a substitute, and calls that doctrine a lie which keeps men from the gospel. He accepts the word in its older English sense of at-one-ment: Jesus is our atonement because through him we are reconciled to God, not God to us. Atonement is a making-up and a bringing together, which only the sinner himself can make, and Christ is its prince, who makes us make it. Sacrifice likewise is turned round. God is not the receiver of sacrifice but its giver: he has always been sacrificing himself for his creatures, and the whole life of Jesus was the offering of himself to the Father. Men’s sacrifices were their own invention, borne with until they could learn better. The true sacrifice asked of us is the self and the will, given back to God in freedom. Vicarious sacrifice and imputed righteousness he calls legal fiction.

Shades of meaning

  1. A making-up that the sinner must make. Punishment that only destroyed the sinner would leave the wrong unmended. “There must be an atonement, a making-up, a bringing together—an atonement which, I say, cannot be made except by the man who has sinned” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”). See justice and punishment.

  2. At-one-ment: man reconciled to God. He disbelieves the atonement as his questioner means it, “for it would be to believe a lie, and a lie which is to blame for much non-acceptance of the gospel in this and other lands” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”). But in the sense the word had for the translators of the Bible, “I believe in the atonement, call it the a-tone-ment, or the at-one-ment, as you please. I believe that Jesus Christ is our atonement; that through him we are reconciled to, made one with God. There is not one word in the New Testament about reconciling God to us; it is we that have to be reconciled to God” (same). His business is not a treatise on the atonement but “to persuade men to be atoned to God” (same). See forgiveness and oneness.

  3. The creative atonement. “The work of Jesus Christ on earth was the creative atonement, because it works atonement in every heart. He brings and is bringing God and man, and man and man, into perfect unity” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”). What Christ did was to bring the Father to us: “Did he not bring the Father to us, let us look on our eternal Sire in the face of his true son, that we might have that in our hearts which alone could make us love him—a true sight of him?” (same). And he overcame evil by bearing it: “Did he not foil and slay evil by letting all the waves and billows of its horrid sea break upon him, go over him, and die without rebound” (same).

  4. The prince of the atonement, who makes us make it. Every repentant child longs to make atonement to the one he has wronged, and Jesus is the power behind that longing: “He is the head and leader, the prince of the atonement. He could not do it without us, but he leads us up to the Father’s knee: he makes us make atonement” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”). So the injured neighbour is to be paid back: “Have I injured anyone? With him to aid my justice, new risen with him from the dead, shall I not make good amends?” (same). See repentance and neighbour.

  5. Vicarious sacrifice as legal fiction. “To believe in a vicarious sacrifice, is to think to take refuge with the Son from the righteousness of the Father; to take refuge with his work instead of with the Son himself; to take refuge with a theory of that work instead of the work itself” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”). The righteousness of God is “so far from being a thing built on the rubbish heap of legal fiction called vicarious sacrifice, or its shadow called imputed righteousness, that only the child with the child-heart” can understand it (Unspoken Sermons, “Righteousness”). Men who could not believe in plain forgiveness “invented a satisfaction for sin which was an insult to God. He sought no satisfaction, but an obedient return to the Father” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”). See righteousness and law.

  6. Trust in the atonement instead of in the Father. Faith is “the leaving of your trust in men, in money, in opinion, in character, in atonement itself, and doing as he tells you” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth in Jesus”). “He who trusts in the atonement instead of in the father of Jesus Christ, fills his fancy with the chimeras of a vulgar legalism, not his heart with the righteousness of God” (The Hope of the Gospel, “God’s Family”). See faith.

  7. God the giver of sacrifice. “God provides the sacrifice; the sacrifice is himself. He is always, and has ever been, sacrificing himself to and for his creatures. It lies in the very essence of his creation of them” (Unspoken Sermons, “Life”). The direction of the old altar is reversed: “We sacrifice to God!—it is God who has sacrificed his own son to us; there was no way else of getting the gift of himself into our hearts” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”). Even freedom is a divine sacrifice: “God sacrifices his will to man that man may become such as himself, and give all to the truth” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Final Unmasking”). See Father and suffering.

  8. The lifelong sacrifice of Jesus. The cry on the cross commending his spirit “was only a summing up of what he had been doing all his life. He had been offering this sacrifice, the sacrifice of himself, all the years, and in thus sacrificing he had lived the divine life” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Hands of the Father”). The sacrifice is not one act at the end but a life of loving words and healing deeds. See Son and obedience.

  9. Men’s sacrifices, borne with. The doctrine of substitution was permitted to last “On the same principle on which God took up and made use of the sacrifices men had, in their lack of faith, invented as a way of pleasing him” (Unspoken Sermons, “Justice”). God accepted them “until he could get them to see” that he does not care for such things (same). The bloody sacrifice came from a troubled conscience, and the same consciousness of offence “is still at work in the minds of most who call themselves Christians” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Fear of God”). See fear.

  10. The sacrifice of self and will. “The self is given to us that we may sacrifice it; it is ours that we like Christ may have somewhat to offer—not that we should torment it, but that we should deny it” (Unspoken Sermons, “Self-Denial”). God stands away from us so that our will may be given in freedom, “found our own first in its loving sacrifice to him” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Eloi”). Ordinary love can be sacrifice too: the poor man has ten houses where he would be welcomed, while the rich man may have no refuge, so ask which is richer, the man without refuge or “he for whose necessity a hundred would sacrifice comfort?” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Heirs of Heaven and Earth”). See self-denial and will.

Neighbouring words

  • justice (the frame of the argument). Atonement is discussed inside MacDonald’s account of justice as setting things right, not paying a debt.
  • punishment (what atonement is not). Punishment serves the making-up; it is never itself the satisfaction.
  • forgiveness (its ground). Satisfaction was invented by those who could not believe in clear forgiveness.
  • reconciliation (its meaning). At-one-ment is reconciling men to God, never God to men.
  • righteousness (what the fiction hides). Imputed righteousness is the shadow of vicarious sacrifice.
  • self-denial (the sacrifice asked of us). The self is given to be offered.
  • obedience (the true return). God sought no satisfaction but an obedient return to the Father.

Scripture. We also joy in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom we have now received the atonement (Romans 5:11, the one place the KJV uses the word). All things are of God, who hath reconciled us to himself by Jesus Christ (2 Corinthians 5:18). I in them, and thou in me, that they may be made perfect in one (John 17:23). Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire (Psalm 40:6). Present your bodies a living sacrifice (Romans 12:1).
