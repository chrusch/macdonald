A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

truth

true

Frequency in the non-fiction: truth 775; true 664; true man 41.

In brief. For MacDonald truth rises through levels. At the bottom is fact, the correctness of a statement. Above that is the truth of a thing, what it is for and what of God it shows. Highest is the truth of a person, a man being what he was made to be in his relations to God and other men. Christ is the Truth in that highest sense, and a man becomes true by doing the truth, not by holding true opinions.

Shades of meaning

  1. Truth above fact. “The Truth” begins by setting the two apart: a statement about which side of the street he walked on concerns a fact, and “The correct word would be a fact, not a truth” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). Whether the speaker believes what he says, though, is “a question of truth: he is either a pure soul, so far as this thing witnesses, or a false soul” (same paragraph, “The Truth”).

  2. The truth of a thing. A created thing’s truth is its meaning, its end, and its showing of God: “The truth of a thing, then, is the blossom of it, the thing it is made for, the topmost stone set on with rejoicing” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). “The truth of the sky is what it makes us feel of the God that sent it out to our eyes” (“The Truth”). Of a mother shown a flower by her child: “here to her is no mere fact; here is no law of nature; here is a truth of nature, the truth of a flower—a perfect thought from the heart of God” (“The Truth”).

  3. Truth of relation. The truths that matter most are the facts of relation, to God and to other men: “The facts of human relation, then, are truths indeed, and of awfullest import” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). The highest is “the relation in which man stands to the source of his being—his will to the will whence it became a will” (“The Truth”). Treated only as ideas, even these become facts again: whoever handles them “as objects of thought” treats “them as facts and not as truths” (same paragraph).

  4. A true man; being a truth. The adjective true most characteristically describes a person who is what he was made to be. “When a man is, with his whole nature, loving and willing the truth, he is then a live truth” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). The man who lives for himself “is in himself a lie”, and his sufferings are “a painful suasion to be himself, to be a truth” (“The Truth”).

  5. Christ the Truth. “The obedient Jesus is Jesus the Truth. He is true and the root of all truth and development of truth in men” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). He is “the Truth, the one thing alone that can be known!” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth in Jesus”).

  6. God as the truth of everything. In “Kingship” the sense is stated in short sentences: “The truth is God; the witness to the truth is Jesus. The kingdom of the truth is the hearts of men. The bliss of men is the true God. The thought of God is the truth of everything” (Unspoken Sermons, “Kingship”).

  7. Doing the truth. Truth that is only known and not lived turns to its opposite: “The man who knows these things, and but knows them; the man who sees them to be true, and does not order life and action, judgment and love by them, is of the worst of lying” (Unspoken Sermons, “Kingship”). Obedience and divinity meet here: Obedience and Service are “both a doing of the truth” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Child in the Midst”).

  8. Bearing witness to the truth. Christ’s kingship is the bearing of witness, and so is the disciple’s: “A man content to bear no witness to the truth is not in the kingdom of heaven” (Unspoken Sermons, “Kingship”). See king, kingship.

  9. Truth in art. In the essays the word keeps its theological charge. A fairy tale “cannot help having some meaning; if it have proportion and harmony it has vitality, and vitality is truth” (A Dish of Orts, “The Fantastic Imagination”).

In the fiction and poetry. Senses 4 and 7 run through the novels: “A man can obey before he is sure, and except he obey the command he knows to be right, wherever it may come from, he will never be sure. To find the truth, man or woman must be true” (The Elect Lady, ch. XXX). The narrator of The Marquis of Lossie remarks “that all the power of a lie comes from the truth; it has none in itself” (The Marquis of Lossie, ch. XXXIII, “Lies”). And in the Diary of an Old Soul truth is God’s will: “Thy will is truth—’tis therefore fate, the strong” (Diary of an Old Soul, January).

Neighbouring words

  • fact (the lower level). See sense 1 and fact.
  • lie, false (opposite). For MacDonald a lie is first a false being and only then a false statement (sense 4).
  • know, see (the faculties that receive it). “To see a truth, to know what it is, to understand it, and to love it, are all one” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Consuming Fire”). See know.
  • do, obey (the way to it). See sense 7 and obedience.
  • imagination (the faculty that finds the truth of a thing). “Truth in a man’s imagination is the power to recognize this truth of a thing” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”). See imagination.
  • opinion, doctrine (substitutes). “I do not attempt to change your opinions; if they are wrong, the obedience alone on which I insist can enable you to set them right” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth in Jesus”).
  • king, kingship (the office of truth). See sense 8.
  • free, freedom (consequence). “Then I am free, in that I am true—which means one with the Father” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth”).
  • idea, thought of God (source). “The thought of God is the truth of everything” (Unspoken Sermons, “Kingship”).

Scripture. John 14:6, I am the way, the truth, and the life; John 18:37, To this end was I born … that I should bear witness unto the truth, the text of “Kingship”; John 8:32, the truth shall make you free; John 3:21, he that doeth truth cometh to the light (all KJV).
