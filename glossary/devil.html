A Glossary of MacDonald’s Terms

devil

Satan · the evil one · devils · possession

Frequency in the corpus: devil, devils and Satan in about 100 paragraphs. The main sources are “The Temptation in the Wilderness” in Unspoken Sermons and “VII. The Casting Out of Devils” in Miracles, with “God’s Family” in The Hope of the Gospel.

In brief. MacDonald speaks of Satan as a real adversary of truth, humanity and God, the lord of misrule, but he gives him little room. He reads the temptation in the wilderness as a parable told by Jesus, not as the visit of a visible demon who quoted Scripture, and he finds the substance of serving Satan in very human things: knowing better and doing worse, leading men toward an end that is not the best, flattering a nation’s pride. He keeps apart the question of possession from the question whether a personal devil exists, and he thinks the old account of possessing spirits fits the Gospel records better than disease alone, though he will not be dogmatic. Men make themselves the devil’s children by dividing and separating; whoever destroys the devil’s power is on Christ’s side. Torture is what one expects of Satan, healing what one expects of God. Satan is foiled when a man joins God.

Shades of meaning

  1. The adversary of Truth, Humanity and God. In the temptation Satan is “one whom he knew to be the adversary of Truth, of Humanity, of God” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”), and later “the lord of misrule and its pain” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). To hold that a man who repents could not be forgiven would mean “that Satan has overcome; and that Jesus, amongst his own brothers and sisters in the image of God, has been less strong than the adversary, the destroyer” (Unspoken Sermons, “It Shall Not be Forgiven”). He calls that teaching a doctrine of devils. See forgiveness.

  2. The temptation as parable, not a visible demon. The inward experience of Jesus could be told to the disciples only in a parable. He asks whether anyone who reads the story as plain narrative is “prepared to believe, as the story, so interpreted, indubitably gives us to understand, that a visible demon came to our Lord and, himself the prince of worldly wisdom, thought, by quoting Scripture after the manner of the priests, to persuade a good man to tempt God” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). “How could Satan be so foolish?” (same). “Told as a parable, it is as full of meaning as it would be bare if received as a narrative” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). See temptation and symbol.

  3. Satan quotes Scripture as authority. “Satan quotes Scripture as a verbal authority; our Lord meets him with a Scripture by the truth in which he regulates his conduct” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). The stone that would be bread is Satan’s: “The Father did not give him a stone when he asked for bread. It was Satan that brought the stone and told him to provide for himself” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). And at the start of the ministry, “Dare and do, for God is with thee,” the devil says, and the king of his brothers answers that he will wait (Unspoken Sermons, “The Eloi”). See temptation and faith.

  4. The service of Satan in ordinary ambition. He asks “whether to know better and do not so well, is not a serving of Satan;—whether to lead men on in the name of God as towards the best when the end is not the best, is not a serving of Satan;—whether to flatter their pride by making them conquerors of the enemies of their nation instead of their own evils, is not a serving of Satan” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). When Peter tried to turn him from Jerusalem, Jesus gave him “the same kind of answer that he now gave to Satan, calling him Satan too” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Temptation in the Wilderness”). See king and will.

  5. Possession kept apart from the devil. “The question concerning the nature of what is called possession has nothing whatever to do with that concerning the existence or nonexistence of a personal and conscious power of evil, the one great adversary of the kingdom of heaven, commonly called Satan, or the devil” (Miracles, “VII. The Casting Out of Devils”). To read the cases as disease seems to him “nothing unchristian” (Miracles, “VII. The Casting Out of Devils”), since every disease may be seen as an inroad of evil powers, yet he thinks it does not fit the record. He offers a supposition: spirits “who, having once had bodies such as ours, and having abused the privileges of embodiment, are condemned for a season to roam about bodiless, ever mourning the loss of their capacity for the only pleasures they care for” (Miracles, “VII. The Casting Out of Devils”). See miracle and resurrection · the body.

  6. Torture of Satan, healing of God. Those who were cured “had no doubt that he was fighting Satan on his usurped ground. Torture was what might be expected of Satan; healing what might be expected of God” (Miracles, “VII. The Casting Out of Devils”). Jesus allowed that others cast out devils too: “He knew that all good is of God, and not of the devil. All were with him who destroyed the power of the devil” (Miracles, “VII. The Casting Out of Devils”). The help of God is ever “setting them free whom Satan hath bound” (Miracles, “IV. Miracles of Healing Unsolicited”). See suffering and salvation.

  7. Children of the evil one by division. Those whose influence is to divide “make themselves the children of the evil one: born of God and not of the devil, they turn from God, and adopt the devil their father” (The Hope of the Gospel, “God’s Family”). No one is born the devil’s child. Opinion does not decide it: a man “holding doctrines of devils, is yet a child of God” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth in Jesus”) if he trusts God, while the man of correct formulas without a daily walk with God “is of the world, yea, of the devil” (same). See oneness and opinion.

  8. The devil’s inventions in society. Work for wages is God’s order. “The devil is not the inventor of this society; he has invented the notion of a certain degradation in work, a still greater in wages; and following this up, has constituted a Society after his own likeness, which despises work, leaves it undone, and so can claim its wages without disgrace” (The Hope of the Gospel, “The Right Hand and the Left”). “Slavery is a bad thing and of the devil, yet mutual jealousy and contempt are worse” (Miracles, “VI. Miracles Granted to the Prayer of Friends”). See work.

  9. Preferring a devil to God. Men far from God fear him because he is doing with them what they do not desire: “they must prefer a devil, because of his supreme selfishness, to a God who will die for his creatures” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Fear of God”). See fear and self.

  10. Satan foiled when man joins God. “God is in contest with the gates of hell that open in the man, and can but hold his own; when the man joins him, then is Satan foiled” (Unspoken Sermons, “The Truth in Jesus”). See obedience and hell.

In the fiction and poetry. In Lilith (1895) evil is given a body in Lilith herself and in the great Shadow she serves; the story turns on Lilith’s long refusal to open her clenched hand, and ends with her at last consenting to sleep in Adam’s house of the dead until she wakes. (General knowledge, not drawn from the corpus.) The figure fits the sermons: evil is shown as a will that holds on to itself, not as a power equal to God.

Neighbouring words

  • temptation and tempter (his work). The temptation in the wilderness is the main place he is met, read as parable.
  • possession and unclean spirit (a separate question). Bodiless spirits craving the pleasures of the body.
  • adversary and destroyer (his titles). Adversary of truth, humanity and God.
  • division and strife (how men become his children). The sowers of strife adopt the devil as father.
  • selfishness (his nature). Men prefer him for his supreme selfishness.
  • hell (his gates in man). God contends with the gates of hell that open in the man.

Scripture. Then was Jesus led up of the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted of the devil (Matthew 4:1). Get thee hence, Satan: for it is written, Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God (Matthew 4:10). If Satan cast out Satan, he is divided against himself (Matthew 12:26). Ye are of your father the devil (John 8:44). For this purpose the Son of God was manifested, that he might destroy the works of the devil (1 John 3:8).
